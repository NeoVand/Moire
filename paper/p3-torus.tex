\section{Where you are in every family}
\label{sec:torus}

Stand at a point $x$ on the pair of rulers. You are, say, three tenths of the
way from tick 40 to tick 41 of family A, and seven tenths of the way from
tick 36 to tick 37 of family B. Those two fractions, $0.3$ and $0.7$, are
where you are \emph{within the current member} of each family, and they are
all a pattern of coincidences can depend on: whether ticks coincide at $x$ is
a question about the fractions, not about the numbers 40 and 36.

\begin{definition}[The torus of counts]
\label{def:torus}
For $K$ families with counts $\cnt_1,\dots,\cnt_K$ the \emph{state} of the
superposition at $x$ is the point
\[
  \Phi(x)=\big(\cnt_1(x),\dots,\cnt_K(x)\big)\bmod 1,
\]
the fractional part of every count at once. For two families $\Phi(x)$ is a
point of the unit square. Walking off the right edge of the square (family A
completes a member) brings you back in at the left edge at the same height,
and walking off the top brings you back in at the bottom: the square with
its opposite edges glued is a \emph{torus}, written $\tor^2$, and $\Phi$ is
a path on it.
\end{definition}

\begin{lookup}{the torus as a square with its edges glued}
What to look up: \emph{why a square with opposite edges identified is a
torus}, and what a function on the torus is. The one consequence this paper
uses: a function on the torus is the same thing as a function $I(x_1,x_2)$ of
two real variables that repeats with period one in each variable
separately. Nothing about the torus's shape in three dimensions is ever
used; only the gluing.
\end{lookup}

The state is the geometry. The appearance is a separate thing, and the
separation is the first idea worth having.

\begin{definition}[The picture on the torus]
\label{def:picture}
What the superposition \emph{looks like} at $x$ --- how much ink, how loud,
whether the detector fires --- depends on $x$ only through $\Phi(x)$, by a
fixed rule $I$ that knows nothing about $x$: the \emph{picture on the torus}.
The superposition is the composite
\[
  S \;=\; I\circ\Phi,\qquad S(x)=I\big(\Phi(x)\big).
\]
\end{definition}

The formula says: look up where you are in every family, then look up what
that state looks like. For two printed gratings $I(x_1,x_2)$ is ``ink if
$x_1$ is within the stroke of a line of A, or $x_2$ within a line of B'' ---
a picture on the torus made of two bands, one across and one up, and their
crossing. For two click trains into a coincidence detector $I(x_1,x_2)$ is
``fire if $x_1$ and $x_2$ are both within a click's width of zero'', a small
square at the corner of the torus. Everything geometric about a superposition
--- pitches, spacings, the bend in a ruler, a drift in a clock --- lives in
$\Phi$. Everything about appearance --- ink, click shape, brightness,
whether inks multiply or lights add --- lives in $I$. Two superpositions with
the same $\Phi$ differ only in their picture, and two with the same picture
differ only in their geometry. The rest of the paper is a list of things that
are properties of $\Phi$, things that are properties of $I$, and things that
are properties of the way an observer averages one over the other.

\subsection{Combined counts, and which of them are slow}
\label{sec:slow}

The difference $D=\cnt_A-\cnt_B$ of Figure~\ref{fig:rulers} is itself a
count: it rises by one per period of the bands, and the bands are its
members. So is the sum $\cnt_A+\cnt_B$, and so is any combination
\[
  k\cdot\Phi \;=\; k_1\cnt_1+k_2\cnt_2+\dots+k_K\cnt_K,
  \qquad k=(k_1,\dots,k_K)\ \text{whole numbers},
\]
a whole number of one count plus a whole number of another. The formula says:
combined counts are made of the counts by integer recipes, and the recipe is
the vector $k$. We write $k=(1,-1)$ for the difference, $(1,1)$ for the sum,
$(2,-1)$ for twice the first count minus the second. Every recipe is a
candidate pattern, all of them at once. Which ones show is a matter of how
fast each changes.

\begin{definition}[Slow and fast; the heterodyne ratio]
\label{def:eta}
At a point $x$ each count has a local rate, the derivative $\nabla\cnt_i(x)$
(a number on a line, a vector in the plane). The rate of a combined count is
$\nabla(k\cdot\Phi)=\sum_i k_i\nabla\cnt_i$. For a combination of two
families, $k=(a,b)$, its \emph{heterodyne ratio} is
\[
  \het_{a,b}(x)\;=\;\frac{\lvert a\,\nabla\cnt_1+b\,\nabla\cnt_2\rvert}
                        {\tfrac12\,\lvert a\,\nabla\cnt_1-b\,\nabla\cnt_2\rvert},
\]
the rate of the combination against the average rate of the two members
that form it. A combination is \emph{slow} when $\het$ is small; we call
$\het<\tfrac14$ the \emph{beat regime}, and it is where every pattern in
this paper lives.
\end{definition}

The formula says: count how many members go by per band. On the rulers of
Figure~\ref{fig:rulers} the difference has $\het=\tfrac{1}{9.5}$, about nine
and a half members per band, and the sum has $\het\approx2$, no members per
band at all: the sum is fast and the difference is slow. Turn one ruler
around so that its count runs the other way, and the sum becomes the slow
one; nothing in the theory prefers a difference. The threshold $\tfrac14$,
four members per band, is where a pattern of coincidences stops being one:
below it the bands are wider than the members that make them, and above it
there is no ``band'', only members. The ratio is a number at every point of
the plane, so it can be drawn as a map, and the tool of
\S\ref{sec:instrument} draws it: bright where no pattern can form, dark
where one must.

For families with many harmonics --- strokes, clicks, square waves, anything
with edges --- higher recipes matter too. A pair of rulers with ten and
nineteen divisions to the inch has a slow combination $(2,-1)$: twice the
first count minus the second rises by one per inch. That is the
\emph{octave} beat, and \S\ref{sec:selection} is about when it shows and when
it cannot.

\begin{tryit}{the map of where a beat can form}
In \Moire\ (\S\ref{sec:instrument}), make two layers of parallel lines a few
percent apart in pitch and turn on the \emph{ratio} view. The frame darkens
where $\het<\tfrac14$: the bands you see in the ordinary view sit exactly
inside the dark region, and as you change the pitches the dark region
predicts where the bands go before they get there. Rotate one layer: the
dark region moves to where the \emph{sum} is slow, and the bands follow.
\end{tryit}
