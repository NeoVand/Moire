\section{When counting fails}
\label{sec:counting}

Everything so far took the count for granted. Here is what it is, exactly.
A family is a set of members with a numbering, and the count at a point $x$
is the number $c$ such that $x$ lies on the interpolated member $c$: the
solution of an equation
\[
  F(x,c)=0,\qquad\text{``$x$ is on member $c$''.}
\]
For a ruler of pitch $T$, $F(x,c)=x/T-c$, and the solution is
$\cnt(x)=x/T$. For concentric rings a distance $s$ apart, $F(x,c)=|x|-cs$
and $\cnt(x)=|x|/s$. For a family of rays from a point, member $c$ is the
ray at angle $c$ times the angle between rays, and the solution is the
polar angle of $x$ divided by that step --- which is where the trouble
starts. Whether $F(x,c)=0$ has a solution, and one solution, that depends
smoothly on $x$, is not automatic. It can happen in three ways, and two of
them are failures.

\begin{lookup}{the implicit function theorem, and winding}
What to look up: \emph{the implicit function theorem}. The quantity
$\partial F/\partial c$ is the rate at which $F$ changes when the member
index is nudged and the point $x$ is held still (a \emph{partial
derivative}). The one consequence used: wherever $\partial F/\partial c\ne0$,
the equation $F(x,c)=0$ defines $c$ as a smooth function of $x$ near that
point; where $\partial F/\partial c=0$ it may not. And \emph{winding
number}: walk once round a point and the angle you make with it gains a
full turn, which no continuous choice of angle can undo.
\end{lookup}

Two words before the theorem. A \emph{walking family} is a nest of curves
--- circles, squares, hexagons --- each grown by a step $s$, displaced by a
step $\delta$ and turned by a step $\theta$ more than the last; it is what
an artist draws when the rings are not concentric. And the three cases
below are called \emph{rungs}, because a family can be lifted from the
first to the second by a field and never to the third, which is the last
paragraph of the section. The theorem is about families in the plane: a
family in time, like the click trains of \S\ref{sec:whosees}, has no loops
to wind around and members that never cross, so its count is always a
formula.

\begin{theorem}[Trichotomy]
\label{thm:trichotomy}
The count of a family in the plane exists in exactly one of three ways.
\emph{As a formula:} a single smooth function on the whole plane (the exact
rung: lines, rings, polygons, spirals, waves). \emph{Up to a whole number:}
a function on the plane with a point removed, which gains a whole number
each time a loop goes round the point (the winding rung: the rays are the
prototype, and a family built on the polar angle has a \emph{defect} at
the point, with a \emph{charge} that is the whole number gained). \emph{Only
as a search:} where two members pass through the same point at the same
interpolated index, $\partial F/\partial c=0$, the count branches, no
relabelling repairs it, and the nearest member must be found by looking
(the fold rung: the walking families).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Where $\partial F/\partial c\ne0$ the lookup gives $c$ locally as a smooth
function of $x$. The local pieces glue into one function on the plane
exactly when carrying a value round any loop brings it back to itself; if
it comes back changed, the change is a whole number (the index of the same
member), it depends only on which points the loop encloses, and it is the
winding of the family about those points. Where $\partial F/\partial c=0$
two members meet $x$ at the same index to first order --- the equation has
a double root in $c$, like $c^2=x$ at $x=0$ --- and just past such a point
the equation has two solutions on one side and none on the other; the
local pieces join at a crease instead of overlapping, and no choice of
labels on a cover of the plane makes the count a single function again.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Defects.}
Add to a family's count the polar angle about a point divided by a full
turn, times a whole number $q$. Nothing about the family's members changes
away from the point, but the count now gains $q$ around any loop that
encloses it, and so does the difference $D$ against an unmodified twin. The
bands of that pair are the level lines of $D$, and a level line has to
leave a loop as often as it enters --- unless the value of $D$ is not the
same after one circuit, in which case $q$ lines that enter never leave.
So $q$ bands \emph{end} inside any loop round the point, and, since winding
adds, the signed number of endings inside any loop is $q$ times the number
of enclosed points. That is the fork grating of
optics~\cite{NyeBerry1974Dislocations} --- a grating with a line that
forks, the signature of a wave that winds --- built from a count rather
than a wavefront, and the count says one more thing: near the point the
added count changes at a rate $q/2\pi r$, so the heterodyne ratio of the
beat is $\het=qs/2\pi r$ for a family of pitch $s$, which reaches the edge
of the beat regime, $\het=\tfrac14$, at $r^\star=2qs/\pi$; inside that
core the beat is not slow enough to be a band. Measured on \defProbes\
loops with charges up to \defChargeMax: the count of endings is exact, and
the core radius holds to within $\defCoreWorst$ (script
\texttt{defects.mjs}; Figure~\ref{fig:defects}).

\paragraph{Folds.}
A walking family's members are the fronts of something that spreads at
speed $s$ from a source that moves by $\delta$ and turns by $\theta$ per
member, and the family folds exactly where a front's normal speed vanishes
--- which is the theorem's $\partial F/\partial c=0$, written for a front:
the next member no longer advances past the last at that point. Two
consequences have closed forms, in terms of the shape's \emph{gauge}
$\gamma$: $\gamma(v)$ is the length of a vector $v$ measured in the shape's
own units, the size of the copy of the shape that just reaches $v$, so that
for circles $\gamma$ is ordinary length. A front that only moves folds if
and only if the source is \emph{supersonic}: it moves faster than it
spreads, $\gamma(-\delta)>s$, and the fold is the shock along the wake. A
front that only turns folds every shape but the circle, at the radius
$s/(\theta\sin(\pi/k))$ for the regular $k$-gon, where a vertex of one
member first crosses the next. Onsets measured by brute force on families
that never saw the calculus are within \foldWorstOnsetPct\% of these radii
(script \texttt{foldlaw.mjs}; Figure~\ref{fig:foldlaw}). Beyond the fold
the count is multivalued, the number of branches through a point grows
with the distance from the centre, and no formula exists: a rational turn
$2\pi a/b$ splits the family into $b$ nests that can each be solved, and
any other turn is shown in the companion paper (\S\ref{sec:instrument}) to
be beyond any formula in $x$. Searching for the nearest member is the price
of a family whose count has folded, and the instrument pays it with a
certificate --- an interval of member indices that provably contains every
member near the pixel --- rather than a guess.

One thing a field cannot do closes the taxonomy. A \emph{field} added to a
count --- any function of position, including one like the polar angle
that is defined only up to whole turns --- replaces $F(x,c)$ by
$F(x,c-\phi(x))$: it shifts the solution $c$ by $\phi(x)$ and leaves
$\partial F/\partial c$ alone. So a field can move a family within its rung
or lift it from the exact rung to the winding rung by a whole number, and it
can never fold one. Folding is a property of the members' geometry; winding
is a property of the count; and the third pattern, being a function of
counts, inherits the winding and ends where the count does.

\begin{tryit}{a count that winds, and one that folds}
In \Moire, take a single family of lines and type the polar angle divided
by a full turn into its field. One line now ends at the centre, and a
second family of the same pitch laid over it shows one band ending there:
charge one. Multiply the field by three and three bands end. Then take a
family of hexagonal rings and give each member a small turn relative to the
last: the nest is clean near the centre and folds where the theorem says,
at radius $s/(\theta\sin(\pi/6))$, and the \emph{ratio} view goes blank
inside the defect's core.
\end{tryit}
