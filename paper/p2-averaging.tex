\section{Law II: averaging, the envelope as a conditional expectation}
\label{sec:averaging}

A moir\'e is a slow character, but a drawing is not a character. It is
$I\circ\Phi$, every character at once, the carriers loudest, and what an
observer sees when it cannot resolve the carriers is an average. This section
is about what an average of a drawing is. The answer is exact, and it is the
same answer whether the average is taken by the tool's sweep, by a long
exposure, by a defocused lens, or by a retina: a conditional expectation of the
ink potential on a subtorus that the counting map alone determines. The
observer contributes only the potential it averages.

\subsection{The sweep is a sharp projection}
\label{sec:sweep}

The tool's envelope view advances every layer's phase along a schedule and
averages. On the torus that is translation along a line.

\begin{theorem}[The envelope is a conditional expectation, and a sharp one]
\label{thm:envelope}
For a schedule $w\in\Z^K$ let $\mathcal E_w I(x)=\int_0^1 I(x+uw)\,du$. Then
\[
  \widehat{\mathcal E_w I}(k)\;=\;\hat I(k)\cdot\mathbf 1[k\cdot w=0],
  \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
  \mathcal E_w I \;=\; \E\big[\,I\,\big|\,L_w\,\big],\quad L_w=\{k:k\cdot w=0\}:
\]
one period of an integer schedule projects exactly onto the characters
annihilated by $w$, with no leakage.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expand $I=\sum_k\hat I(k)\,e^{2\pi i\,k\cdot x}$. Translation by $uw$
multiplies the $k$-th term by $e^{2\pi i u\,(k\cdot w)}$, and
$\int_0^1 e^{2\pi i u m}\,du$ is $1$ for $m=0$ and $0$ for every other integer
$m$. Because $w$ is integral, $k\cdot w$ is an integer. The right-hand side is
the orthogonal projection onto the span of the characters in $L_w$, which is
the conditional expectation onto the functions invariant under the closed
subgroup $\overline{\{uw\}}$.
\end{proof}

That $w$ is a vector of integers is not a convenience. It is the reason there
is no leakage: a schedule with a non-integer coordinate multiplies each
character by a sinc rather than an indicator, and the envelope carries a little
of everything. The rank of the projection is the schedule's to choose.

\begin{corollary}[Four cases]
\label{cor:cases}
\emph{(i) Fringe law.} Two layers on the diagonal $w=(1,1)$: $L_w=\{(a,-a)\}$,
so $\mathcal E_wI$ is a function of $D=\idx_1-\idx_2$ alone,
$\mathcal E_wI(x)=\Phi(x_1-x_2)$ with $\Phi(\Delta)=\int_0^1 I(v,v-\Delta)\,dv$.
For two strokes this is the saturating tent: flat while one stroke lies inside
the other, rising with unit slope, saturating once they clear.
\emph{(ii) Pivot.} A schedule with rationally independent coordinates has
$L_w=\{0\}$: the average is $\hat I(0)$, the mean ink with the phases
independent, and expanding contrast about it amplifies exactly
$\mathcal E_wI-\hat I(0)$, the correlations $L_w$ carries.
\emph{(iii) Zero sums.} The diagonal $w=(1,\dots,1)$ has $L_w=\{k:\sum_i k_i=0\}$,
so it preserves every pairwise difference and every zero-sum ternary at once;
only a character with $\sum_i k_i\ne0$ --- a sum beat, a station $(q,-p)$ with
$q\ne p$ --- asks for a schedule of its own, $(|b|,-a\,\mathrm{sgn}\,b)$ on its
pair.
\emph{(iv) Exposure.} A stack animated at rates $r\in\Z^K$ and integrated over
one period is $\mathcal E_r I$ along the drawing: a long exposure is an
envelope, and the shutter chooses $L_r$.
\end{corollary}

Case (iii) is why the tool's envelope holds the diagonal unless a certified
character asks otherwise: the diagonal is the one schedule that keeps every
zero-sum character of every pair and triple in the stack, and a deviation from
it, which serves one character, costs the rest. Case (iv) is the subject of
\S\ref{sec:exposure}.

\subsection{Exactness}
\label{sec:exactness}

Sampling the sweep at $m$ taps computes a Riemann sum whose error is set by
the least-resolved period: a rate-$q$ coordinate slides its layer through $q$
periods, and under about eight taps per period what survives the average is
mostly undersampled carrier. The remedy is not more taps. Along the sweep
every layer's coverage is a stroke profile --- a window with anti-alias ramps,
$1-\operatorname{smoothstep}(h-a,h+a,d)$ in the tool --- composed with the
distance $d(u)$ to the nearest of the three members the phase trio names, and
$d$ is piecewise linear in $u$. So each coverage is piecewise cubic in $u$, and
the places where its piece changes are known in closed form: the nearest
member's window edges $d=h\pm a$, the floor a radial family keeps, the
midpoints between neighbours where the nearest member changes, and the seam
where the slide wraps. A product of such profiles is piecewise polynomial with
known breakpoints, and integrating each piece with a Gauss rule of sufficient
degree is exact.

\begin{theorem}[Exactness]
\label{thm:exact}
For an all-scalar stack every swept profile $\alpha_i(u)$ is piecewise cubic in
$u$ with corners known in closed form from the phase trio, so
$\mathcal E_w I(\Phi(p))$ is piecewise polynomial with known breakpoints and is
integrated in closed form. Certified on \exactScenes\ scenes across twelve
regimes --- pairs and trios on the diagonal and on deviated schedules up to the
rate-12 station, walking families' asymmetric trios, radial floors, sub-pixel
strokes, strokes that nearly touch --- against a \exactTruthTaps-tap
double-precision truth: the shipped Gauss-3 chain is within $\exactWorstErr$
(one part in \exactWorstGrayFrac\ of a gray level), a Gauss-4 pass within
$\exactGaussFourErr$, and the shipped shader within $\exactGpuErr$ of its
double-precision twin. Beside it, \envTaps\ taps err by \exactTapsWorst\ of the
tonal range at the rate-12 station, and the chain beats them
$\exactOverTaps\times$ at worst.
\end{theorem}

The Gauss-4 figure is the proof that the corner list is complete: a missing
corner would leave a cubic straddling two pieces, which no rule integrates
exactly, and the error would not fall from the Gauss-3 figure to
$\exactGaussFourErr$. The shader figure is the twin discipline: the same trios
through the renderer's own code, compiled into a compute pass, against a
JavaScript implementation of the same segmentation in double precision. What
sampling had cost is the last number. The ``annoying envelope artifacts'' that
motivated this were never artifacts of the envelope; they were tap noise, and
they are gone at every zoom, thinness and rate, faster than the sampling they
replaced, because a handful of corners is cheaper than fifty taps.

\subsection{Temporal selection}
\label{sec:exposure}

Case (iv) of Corollary~\ref{cor:cases} is a physical prediction, and it was
not sought: it fell out of the theorem and survived measurement the same day.

\begin{theorem}[Temporal selection]
\label{thm:exposure}
Averaging a stack animated at rates $r\in\Z^K$ over one period keeps exactly
the characters with $k\cdot r=0$. On the $16.4{:}8$ pair's station $(2,-1)$:
rates $(1,2)$ keep it to \exposureFaithfulPct\% of the still's amplitude, while
$(1,1)$ and $(2,1)$ wash it $\exposureSelectivity\times$.
\end{theorem}

  \centering

  as a still, then its long exposures over one period at rates $(1,1)$,
  $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$, each expanded sixfold about its own mean. The still's
  station is a four-percent modulation the page cannot show. Rates $(1,1)$
  keep the zero-sum characters --- here the carrier-scale coefficient $(1,-1)$,
  a fine texture, not a fringe --- and wash the station; rates $(1,2)$ keep

  \texttt{exposure-figs.mjs}.}
  \label{fig:exposure}

Figure~\ref{fig:exposure} is the theorem drawn. A camera pointed at two
gratings sliding past each other records, over its shutter time, an envelope
along the rate vector, and which fringe system it records is a matter of
arithmetic: $k\cdot r=0$. The still cannot show the station at all --- it is a
four-percent modulation under carriers at full contrast --- and the exposure at
rates $(1,2)$ extracts it whole, because that is the schedule the station's
character annihilates. The envelope view is not post-processing. It is what a
slow observer of an animated stack already computes.

\subsection{The observer theorem}
\label{sec:observer}

Theorem~\ref{thm:envelope} says what the sweep computes. The question this
paper set out to answer is why the same object should appear to any observer
that cannot resolve the carriers --- a lens, a film, a retina --- and whether
the third pattern is theirs or the drawing's. It is the drawing's, in two
halves: a statement about invariance, and a statement about windows.

\subsubsection{Invariants of rephasing}

Rephasing layer $i$ by $c_i$ translates the ink potential, $I\mapsto I(\cdot+c)$,
$c\in\tor^K$. A slow observer cannot tell rephasings along the fast directions
apart. What can it see?

\begin{proposition}[The envelope is the universal invariant of rephasing]
\label{prop:invariant}
For a closed subgroup $H\le\tor^K$ let $\E_H I=\int_H I(\cdot+h)\,dh$. Every
continuous linear functional $F$ on ink potentials that is invariant under
translation by $H$ satisfies $F=F\circ\E_H$, and every $G\circ\E_H$ is
invariant: the $H$-invariant linear observables are exactly the linear
observables of the envelope.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$F(I)=\int_H F(I(\cdot+h))\,dh=F\big(\int_H I(\cdot+h)\,dh\big)=F(\E_HI)$,
exchanging a bounded linear map with a Bochner integral over a compact group.
Conversely $\E_H$ is $H$-invariant and idempotent.
\end{proof}

The proposition is the sweep's Theorem~\ref{thm:envelope} read as a
universal property: $\E_H$ is the quotient of the ink by the fast rephasing in
the category of linear observers, and nothing an invariant linear observer
measures escapes it. The word \emph{linear} cannot be dropped. $F(I)=\int I^2$
is invariant under the whole torus and is not a function of $\E[I]$; the
invariants of rephasing among nonlinear functionals of the potential form the
potential's orbit space, far larger than any envelope. But the nonlinear
observers that matter physically are not functionals of the potential at all.
They are functionals of the drawing --- a pointwise front end and a spatial
window --- and for those the right statement is the second half.

\subsubsection{A window is a multiplier on the torus}

\begin{theorem}[A window is a multiplier on the torus]
\label{thm:multiplier}
Let $W\ge0$ be an even window with $\int W=1$ and second moment $m_2$, and
$W_\rho$ its dilate to scale $\rho$. For $\Phi\in C^2$ and
$\sum_k|k||\hat I(k)|<\infty$,
\[
  (D*W_\rho)(p)\;=\;\sum_{k\in\Z^K}\hat I(k)\,\widehat W\!\big(\rho\,J_p^{\top}k\big)\,
  e^{2\pi i\,k\cdot\Phi(p)}\;+\;R_\rho(p),
  \qquad
  |R_\rho(p)|\;\le\;\pi\,\lVert D^2\Phi\rVert\,m_2\,\rho^2\sum_k|k||\hat I(k)|,
\]
and to first order $R_\rho$ is the multiplier's own second derivative in
frequency. Verified: exact to $\obsMultiplierErr$ without curvature; with it,
the second-derivative term leaves $\obsRemainderResidual$ of the remainder.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expand $I$ in characters and write $\Phi(q)=\Phi(p)+J_p(q-p)+E(q)$ with
$|E(q)|\le\tfrac12\lVert D^2\Phi\rVert|q-p|^2$. The window integral of
$e^{2\pi i\,k\cdot J_p(q-p)}$ is $\widehat W(\rho J_p^{\top}k)$, and the factor
$e^{2\pi i\,k\cdot E}$ differs from $1$ by at most $2\pi|k||E|$, which
integrates against $W_\rho$ to $\pi|k|\lVert D^2\Phi\rVert m_2\rho^2$. For the
first-order term, expand $e^{2\pi i k\cdot E}\approx1+2\pi i\,k\cdot E$ with
$E$ quadratic in $q-p$; the integral of a quadratic against
$e^{2\pi i\nu x}W_\rho$ is $-\widehat W_\rho''(\nu)/4\pi^2$.
\end{proof}

The reading is the point. A pooling observer applies to the ink potential
\emph{on the torus} the multiplier $m_p(k)=\widehat W(\rho\,\nabla(k\cdot\Phi)(p))$:
the window's transfer function at each character's own local frequency. When
the local frequencies split around $1/\rho$ --- the fringe regime, $\het\ll1$
--- the multiplier is the indicator of the slow characters up to the window's
leakage, and the observer reports $\E[I\mid\text{slow}](\Phi(p))$: the soft
version of the sweep's sharp projection. The two agree exactly on
$k\cdot J_p=0$ and differ only by what the window leaks. The classical
explanation of moir\'e as what survives a low-pass filter is this statement's
$\rho$-soft special case, made per pixel, with the leakage explicit.

\subsubsection{Universality}

\begin{lemma}[Potential transport]
\label{lem:transport}
For any pointwise $N:\R\to\R$, $N\circ(I\circ\Phi)=(N\circ I)\circ\Phi$.
\end{lemma}

A front-end nonlinearity changes the potential and never the counting map.
Which characters are slow, and where, is a property of $\Phi$; Law~I is
observer-independent, and Law~II applies with $I$ replaced by $N\circ I$.

\begin{theorem}[Universality of the third pattern]
\label{thm:universality}
Every observer that pools before it decides, $O=A_\rho\circ N$ with $N$
pointwise and $A_\rho$ a window, reports at $p$ the potential $N\circ I$
filtered by the multiplier of Theorem~\ref{thm:multiplier}; in the fringe
regime, $O(D)(p)=\E[\,N\circ I\mid L_p\,](\Phi(p))$. The subtorus is
$\Phi$'s alone. An observer chooses only the potential it averages.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:transport} and Theorem~\ref{thm:multiplier} applied to
$N\circ I$, whose coefficients satisfy the summability hypothesis whenever
$N$ is smooth and $I$'s do; anything applied after the window acts on a
function of the result.
\end{proof}

Order matters, and the theorem says how. An observer that pools first and
responds after, $g\circ A_\rho$, sees $g(\E[I\mid L])$: the linear envelope,
regraded, with no new character. An observer that responds first,
$A_\rho\circ N$, sees the envelope of $N\circ I$: the same admissible
characters, different amplitudes, and possibly characters $I$ never carried.
Both are conditional expectations on the same subtorus. That is the precise
sense in which the third pattern is canonical: every observer that pools
before it decides reports an envelope, and only the potential is theirs.

\subsubsection{Reach}

Each corollary below is a prediction the theory was not built to make, and
each is gated in \texttt{observer.mjs}.

\begin{corollary}[Who sees a beat]
\label{cor:whosees}
A printed overlay multiplies transmittances, $I=\prod_i g_i(x_i)$, so every cross
character is present at linear order: a linear observer sees the beat at
$\hat g_1(1)\hat g_2(1)$ (measured \obsPrintedBeat). An additive superposition,
$I=\sum_i g_i(x_i)$, has its spectrum on the coordinate axes: no linear observer
sees a beat (measured $\obsAdditiveBeat$), a squaring front end mints one at
exactly $\tfrac12\hat g_1(1)\hat g_2(1)$ (measured \obsSquaringBeat), and a
saturating one at \obsSaturatingBeat.
\end{corollary}

This is Helmholtz's combination tone and the demodulator's rectifier, said in
the language of characters. Two tones in air, two incoherent projected
gratings, add; their beat has no character of its own, and it is heard or seen
only because an ear or a photoreceptor is not linear before it pools. Two
printed gratings multiply, and their moir\'e is in the ink: a defocused camera
sees it. The distinction is not between kinds of observer but between kinds
of superposition, and the theorem puts it where it belongs, in the support of
$\widehat{N\circ I}$.

\begin{corollary}[Hard ink is observer-proof; soft ink is not]
\label{cor:hardink}
If $I$ takes two values, $N\circ I$ is affine in $I$ and every observer sees
the same envelope up to grading: the $2{:}1$ duty null holds under every front
end (measured $\obsHardNull$). With anti-alias ramps of half-width
\obsReopenRamps\ world units the null holds for every linear observer and a
squaring observer reopens it at $\obsReopenAmps$, linearly in the ramp.
\end{corollary}

The first half is why a drawing on paper has one third pattern: hard ink
leaves an observer nothing to do but grade. The second half is sharper than it
looks. A duty-$\tfrac12$ stroke with symmetric ramps is still exactly
half-wave antisymmetric --- $\operatorname{smoothstep}$ included --- so its
even harmonics vanish and the null survives every linear observer at every
blur; a squaring observer destroys the antisymmetry on the ramps and nowhere
else, which is why the reopened amplitude is linear in the ramp width. A
nonlinear observer of a blurred drawing sees stations a linear one cannot.

\begin{corollary}[Nyquist]
\label{cor:nyquist}
A finite-difference estimate of $D\Phi$ is itself an observer with a two-pixel
window; its multiplier has its first zero at two pixels per member. Closed-form
gradients are the $\rho\to0$ limit, an observer with no window.
\end{corollary}

Figure~\ref{fig:quilt} is the corollary as a defect the tool once had. The
character scan estimated a walking family's index gradient by differencing
the fractional index across one pixel and rounding the unit wraps away, which
is sound while a member spans two pixels and meaningless below; at $1.87$
pixels per member the two layers of the exhibit unwrapped differently in
angular sectors, their sum read as the slow character, and the sweep held
carrier-pitch rims wherever the wrap flipped, in pockets that migrated with
zoom because they were a property of the pixel grid. The cure was not a rule
but a closed form for the gradient of every family, and the corollary says why
no rule could have sufficed.

\subsection{Conditioning is a chart choice}
\label{sec:licence}

\begin{remark}[Conditioning is a chart choice]
\label{rem:licence}
A view that conditions on a subtorus $L_p$ varying with $p$ is $\E[I\mid L_p]$
pointwise but is the envelope of no schedule; where the domains of two
stations meet, the two conditional expectations differ by the characters in
$L_1\,\triangle\,L_2$, and that difference is a seam. Deviation identity is a
decision of the stack, never of the pixel.
\end{remark}

The remark is the second face of a lesson the tool taught twice. On a pair
whose local pitch ratio varies --- a dipole-warped $2.31{:}1$ line pair sweeps
its ratio through $1.67$ to $2.95$ --- in-regime stations tile the whole
frame, $(2,-1)$ over half of it and $(5,-2)$, $(3,-1)$, $(12,-5)$ in pockets
elsewhere, and a view that gave each pocket its own schedule was quilted with
circular seams between textures no render shows. The rule that ended it is the
remark: the stack's nominal pitch ratio certifies at most one deviating
character globally, and every local station rides the diagonal. Measurement is
a layer, with two questions of its own: on which subtorus (which must be
answered once, for the whole chart), and through what window (which is
Nyquist-limited, Corollary~\ref{cor:nyquist}). The third pattern is what
remains once both are answered honestly.
