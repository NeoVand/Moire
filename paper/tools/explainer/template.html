Moiré Fields · a gentle introduction

The Third Pattern

You draw two things. A third thing appears that you never drew. This page is about taking that third thing seriously — seriously enough to prove theorems about it, build an instrument for it, and draw with it on purpose.

twist 3.0° · bands every 153 px

Drag the slider. The two line combs are identical — same spacing, same thickness — and neither of them contains those broad dark bands sweeping across the box. The bands live in the relationship between the combs. Turn one comb a little and the bands swing a lot; that number under the slider is a prediction, not a measurement, and you can watch it stay honest as you drag.

This effect is called moiré, after a rippling silk fabric. You have seen it on a screen door behind a second screen door, on a striped shirt destroying a TV camera, in the shimmer of two chain-link fences. Lord Rayleigh used it in 1874 to test the quality of ruled diffraction gratings; op-artists like Bridget Riley built careers on it; and in 2018 physicists discovered that two sheets of graphene, twisted by exactly 1.1°, make a moiré at the atomic scale that turns the material superconducting. Same mathematics, eleven orders of magnitude apart.

Why a third pattern appears at all

Here is the whole mechanism, in one sentence: where the lines of the two combs land on top of each other, their ink overlaps and the paper stays relatively light; where the lines of one comb fall into the gaps of the other, they blacken the page together. Whether the lines currently align or interleave changes slowly as you move across the page — much more slowly than the lines themselves repeat — so you see slow, fat bands built out of fast, thin lines. Engineers know this as a beat: two guitar strings tuned almost together produce a slow throb at the difference of their frequencies. Moiré is a beat you can look at.

That is the folklore version. The interesting question is what the bands are, exactly — exactly enough to compute with. That question is what the paper this page introduces, Moiré Fields, is about.

A layer is a question, not a picture

Every previous way of making moiré on a computer treats a layer as an image: draw the lines into pixels, draw the other lines into pixels, multiply. The paper's first move is to refuse to do that. A family of curves — the 7th line, the 12th ring, the 40th turn of a spiral — is described instead by two functions that can be asked at any point p of the plane:

φ(p) — the index field: which member of the family are you nearest, fractionally? If φ(p) = 7.3, you are three tenths of the way from ring 7 to ring 8.
d(p) — the distance field: how far is that nearest member? Compare it to a stroke width, and you have ink.

For concentric circles of spacing s, the index field is just φ(p) = ‖p‖ / s — distance from the center, counted in rings. Nothing is ever rasterized: at every pixel, at every zoom, the two functions are simply asked again. The pattern has no resolution, the way a parabola has no resolution.

This sounds like bookkeeping. It is not, because of what happens when you superpose two layers and subtract their index fields.

The one-line theorem

Call the two layers' index fields φ₁ and φ₂, and define D = φ₁ − φ₂. At some points D is a whole number — you are, say, on member 12 of one family and member 9 of the other, so the two curves pass through the same spot and their ink overlaps. The central theorem of the paper says that is the whole story:

The fringe law. The light bands of the superposition are exactly the level sets D ∈ ℤ — the curves where the index difference is a whole number. The dark bands sit at D ∈ ℤ + ½, where the two families interleave and spread their ink furthest. Nothing else about either family survives. The moiré is a contour plot of D.

The locus itself was known in 1964 — physicists called it the indicial equation and used it to read strain off deformed gratings. What the paper adds is everything around it: an exact formula for how much ink the eye averages between the fringes (it is a tent-shaped profile with a flat top, not a sine wave — which is why moiré fringes look so much crisper than a blur would suggest, and why thickening your strokes past a point stops changing the fringes and just darkens the page), a proof that none of it needs the layers to be periodic (spirals count; so do families no formula describes), and a criterion for the question every artist actually has:

Will a fringe even form here?

Two carriers only beat if they are almost the same. Make them too different — cross them at ninety degrees, or give one twice the pitch — and you get a woven texture, not bands. The paper compresses "almost the same" into one number you can compute at every pixel: the heterodyne ratio η, which asks how fast D changes compared with the carriers themselves. Below about ¼, fringes; above it, weave. Because η is itself a field, the instrument can paint a map of it — it can tell you where on the canvas fringes will live before you commit to the parameters. In the ring figure above, the crimson curves stop where η passes ¼: the theorem politely declines to describe the middle, and the middle is indeed where the picture stops being bands and becomes a rosette.

One honest wrinkle, discovered by dragging a slider the day before this page was written. Counting is a convention: you can number a family's members left-to-right or right-to-left and the curves do not care. So the beat between two layers lives in whichever of φ₁ − φ₂ and φ₁ + φ₂ changes more slowly — the classical difference and sum moirés. Where two families' counting directions oppose, the difference ratio screams "no fringe possible!" while a bold sum fringe sits in plain sight. The criterion, correctly stated, takes the minimum of the two. The author of the tool found this because a fringe he could see was being flagged as impossible — which is what instruments are for.

Photographing the ghost

If the fringe field is the real object, you should be able to look at it without the carrier lines in the way. Blurring the image is the obvious move and the wrong one — blur softens the fringes too, and how much blur depends on zoom. The right move is stranger: slide every family through one full period of its own phase and average. Each family sweeps over its own lines — so the lines vanish — but the difference between two families never changes during the slide, so the fringes stand perfectly still while the carrier washes away. Nothing is blurred; the average is over phase, not space.

The tool ships this as a live view called the envelope, and the split images further down this page use it: pattern on top, envelope below, the carrier dissolving into its own ghost at the seam.

The hard case, and the theorem that pays rent

Everything so far had a formula: ask φ, get an answer. Now give ring number n its own personal motion — shift it by nδ, turn it by nθ — which is what op-art actually does. The nest of shapes shears and unwinds into a pinwheel, and the formula dies: no closed-form expression of position tells you which ring is nearest, because every ring lives in its own coordinate frame. The nearest ring must be searched for, over infinitely many candidates, at every pixel, sixty times a second.

Guessing "the ring whose radius matches mine, give or take a few" fails in a spectacular way: the pixels where the guess is wrong are not scattered noise but whole arcs, so whole sides of rings silently vanish from the picture. The paper's answer is a small piece of classical geometry. The "radius" of a square or triangle is a support function, and support functions come with gifts: they can't change faster than distance (a Lipschitz bound), they can't dip below a known fraction of the true radius (cos(π/N), exactly), and they drift per ring at a rate you can compute exactly. Feed those three facts through two inequalities and out comes an interval of ring numbers that provably contains every ring that could possibly matter. Check those; skip the rest of infinity. A second trick skips most of the interval too. The result is certified: the searched answer equals brute force at every single pixel tested — two thousand four hundred parameter settings, zero disagreements — while doing less work than brute force told in advance where to stop. A guarantee, not a budget.

Now run it backwards

Here is the payoff for taking D seriously. The fringe law says fringes are the contours of D. Nobody said D had to be an accident. Type any function f(x, y) — the tool has a little language for this, with automatic differentiation so you never supply a derivative — subtract it from one layer's phase, and the fringes become the contour plot of your function. No contouring algorithm, no marching squares, no polylines: the contours appear because two families of ink coincide there, drawn by the same two distance queries as everything else.

Streamlines of a flow, equipotentials of a dipole, height contours of a surface — if you can write it, the moiré will draw it, live, at any zoom. A century of measurement science used this direction in reverse (interferometers read surface height off fringes); putting it in a drawing tool turns a measurement principle into a pencil.

Why this is worth a paper

Because it is the cleanest small example I know of a big idea: emergence with a complete theory. The bands are not in either layer; they are born from the relationship between layers — and yet, unlike most emergent things, here the emergent object has an exact address (D ∈ ℤ), an exact profile (the tent), an exact existence criterion (η ≤ ¼, minimized over a convention), and an exact price (a certified search interval). You get the wonder and the receipts. The same difference-of-structures is currently the hottest object in condensed-matter physics; here you can hold it, drag it, and watch it answer.

In the paper's own terms, the contributions stack like this: a representation (a layer is two fields, so the pattern has no resolution), a law (fringes are unit level sets of the index difference, tent profile, computable validity map), an algorithm (the support-function certificate that makes the formula-less families interactive), an instrument (a browser studio where all of it is live under the cursor), and an inversion (choose the difference, and the moiré becomes a plotter for arbitrary fields). Each one is ordinary; the loop they close is not.
