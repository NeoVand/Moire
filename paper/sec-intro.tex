\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Draw a comb of parallel lines on tracing paper. Draw another. Lay one over the
other and turn it a few degrees. A third pattern appears --- broad, slow bands
that belong to neither sheet, sweeping across the page as you turn. Nothing was
added. The bands are made of the same ink you already had, rearranged by nothing
but the difference between two things that were almost the same.

This is the oldest computational image, in the sense that it is a computation you
can perform with two pieces of paper and no arithmetic, and it produces a result
that no amount of staring at either input would predict. Rayleigh put it to work in
1874, judging the quality of a ruled grating by the fringes it made against its own
copy~\cite{Rayleigh1874Gratings}; Righi measured the displacement of two gratings
from the motion of their fringes in 1887~\cite{Righi1887Sovrapposizione}. Oster and
Nishijima put it in \emph{Scientific American} in
1963~\cite{Oster1963Moire}, and by 1965 Oster was writing about it as an artistic
medium, next to Riley and Vasarely~\cite{Oster1965Optical}; artists have been
building patterns for it ever since, by hand~\cite{Watkins1982Kinetic} and by
the plate~\cite{Nicolai2010Index}. Amidror gave it a complete Fourier
theory~\cite{Amidror2009Theory}. Hersch's group at EPFL turned it into a design
discipline and then into security printing~\cite{Hersch2004Band, Chosson2014Beating}.
Physics lately rediscovered it at the nanoscale: two graphene sheets twisted by
$1.1^\circ$ form a moir\'e superlattice whose slow beat reorganises the
material's electronic structure into
superconductivity~\cite{Bistritzer2011Moire, Cao2018Magic} --- the same
difference of two dense structures, doing condensed-matter physics.

And yet, if you sit down today and try to \emph{draw} with moir\'e, you find that
the tools do not exist. This paper is about why, and about what we think the
missing representation is.

\subsection{Two traditions, one blind spot}

Graphics has related to moir\'e twice, and the two relationships have almost
nothing to do with each other.

The first is \emph{suppression}. Moir\'e is what undersampling looks like when the
signal is repetitive: a texture at grazing incidence, a shadow map at the wrong
resolution, a halftone screen rescanned. Essentially the whole of texture
filtering exists to prevent it~\cite{Heckbert1986Survey, Zwicker2002EWA}, and it
remains a first-class concern in neural rendering~\cite{Barron2021MipNeRF}. Here
moir\'e is a symptom, and the cure is to remove high frequencies before they can
beat.

The second is \emph{synthesis}. A smaller and much richer literature builds
moir\'e on purpose. Band moir\'e images place a compressed picture in the bands of
a base grating and reveal it by sampling with a line
grating~\cite{Hersch2004Band}. Level-line moir\'e encodes a shape as an elevation
profile, embeds it as local phase shifts, and reveals its contours as fringes that
\emph{beat} when the layers slide~\cite{Chosson2014Beating}. Target-driven phase
modulation solves for two gratings whose $(1,-1)$ interference component
reproduces an arbitrary grayscale image~\cite{Tsai2013Target}. Variational
formulations choose among the many pattern pairs that produce a desired
superposition~\cite{Lebanon2001Variational}. The artistic-screening line makes the
halftone screen itself a designed object~\cite{Ostromoukhov1995Artistic}. Recent
work moves the same physics into micro-optics and curved
fabrication~\cite{Walger2019Moire, Saberpour2020Fabrication}, and into sensing,
where the amplification runs backwards and a moir\'e becomes a sub-millimetre
encoder~\cite{Qiu2023MoireTag, CamposZamora2024MoireWidgets}.

Read across that second tradition and one assumption is universal: \textbf{a layer
is a raster}. It is an image of a grating, possibly warped, and the moir\'e is
what happens when two such images are multiplied. Everything follows from that.
Authoring is offline, because you must solve for and then rasterise the layers ---
Tsai and Chuang report about two minutes per result~\cite{Tsai2013Target}. Playback
is \emph{physical}: you print the two layers on transparencies and slide them, which
is instantaneous but also the only interaction you get. And the pattern has a
resolution, so it has a Nyquist limit, so the act of displaying it can destroy it or
invent it. What the literature does not contain, as far as we can find, is an
interactive tool an artist can draw moir\'e \emph{in}: every synthesis paper above
either solves for a fixed pair of layers or studies a physical superposition, and the
nearest interactive system, FabObscura~\cite{Sethapakdi2025FabObscura}, authors
barrier-grid occlusion rather than interference.

We think the gap is not a missing feature. It is a missing representation.

\subsection{The question instead of the picture}

A layer, stripped of its raster, is a countable family of curves
$\{\gamma_n\}$ --- the $n$th line, the $n$th ring, the $n$th turn of a spiral.
A pixel does not need the family. It needs one number: how far away is the
nearest member? Give it that, threshold it against a stroke width, and you have
ink. Do it for several families and composite. The moir\'e is not computed at all;
it is what the eye does with the composite, just as it is with paper.

So we represent a layer by two scalar fields. The \emph{index field} $\idx(p)$
says which member $p$ sits on, fractionally, so that $\gamma_n=\idx^{-1}(n)$. The
\emph{distance field} $d(p)=\min_n \mathrm{dist}(p,\gamma_n)$ says how far the
nearest one is. Both are functions of a continuous position, evaluated per pixel,
with no grid anywhere (Figure~\ref{fig:traditions}).

This sounds like a small change of bookkeeping. It is not, and the reason is that
the two fields are not redundant. The distance field is what you \emph{render};
the index field is what you \emph{think with}. Our central observation is that the
interference lives entirely in the second one:

\begin{quote}
\itshape The fringes of a superposition are the unit level sets of the difference
of the two index fields.
\end{quote}

Not approximately, and not only for periodic layers. In
Section~\ref{sec:fringe} we make this precise --- the locally averaged ink is a
function of $D=\idx_1-\idx_2$ alone, and that function is a saturating tent rather
than a sinusoid --- state the exact condition under which it holds, and verify it
against $\fringeSamples$ measurements over $\fringeScenes$ pairs of families to a
mean error of $\fringeMeanErr$ in coverage. The condition turns out to be a bound on one local
scalar, the \emph{heterodyne ratio} $\het=|\nabla D| / |\nabla \idx|$, which is
the field-space form of ``the two carriers must be close.'' Because $\het$ is a
field, we can draw it, and tell an artist where in the frame a fringe will form
before they commit to the parameters.

The classical theory reaches this conclusion for locally periodic layers by
Fourier argument, and reaches it asymptotically~\cite{Amidror2009Theory,
Amidror1998Fourier}. The field version is exact, per-pixel, and indifferent to
periodicity: it applies verbatim to an Archimedean spiral over circles, to two
hyperbola families, and to a pencil of rings that is not a geometric transform of
any periodic structure. That last case is where the paper earns its keep.

\subsection{The hard case is the interesting case}

For most families the index field is easy. Parallel lines: $\idx=\langle
p,u\rangle/s$. Circles about a fixed centre: $\idx=\lVert p\rVert/s$. Squares,
triangles, $N$-gons: the same with $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ replaced by that shape's
radial metric. In each case one closed-form scalar function of $p$ has the family
as its level sets, and both fields are $O(1)$. Section~\ref{sec:catalog}
works through all thirteen families we ship; eleven are of this kind.

The families that make the richest interference are not. What op-artists and
pattern designers actually reach for are families whose members are \emph{not}
concentric: each ring displaced a little further than the last, or turned a
little further, so the family as a whole shears, spirals, and finally unwinds into
a pencil of near-parallel curves --- Riley's \emph{Blaze}, a nest of zigzag rings
each turned against the last, is exactly such a family~\cite{Seitz1965Responsive}. We call these \emph{walking families}
(Section~\ref{sec:walking}): member $n$ is the shape of radius $ns+\varphi$,
centred at $\Rot{n\theta}(n\delta)$ and turned by $n\theta$. Two extra
parameters, and the closed form is gone: each curve lives in its own frame, so no
formula in $p$ has these curves as its level sets, and the index field of
\S\ref{sec:field} survives only as the answer to a search. The nearest index is
no longer $\lVert p\rVert/s$, and it can be very far from it.

What we found in practice --- and what we suspect anyone who builds this tool
finds --- is that the obvious response fails in a specific and instructive way.
Guess the index from the radius, check a fixed number of neighbours, take the
minimum. This is wrong, and it is wrong \emph{coherently}: the pixels whose true
nearest member fell outside the guessed neighbourhood form arcs, not speckle, so
whole sides of whole rings disappear. For a long time we believed these
``broken segments'' were a rasterisation bug. They are an inversion bug. And it
is also slow, because the full budget is paid at every pixel whether or not the
answer was the first candidate --- slow enough that these two sliders were, in
our own tool, parameters users learned not to touch.

Section~\ref{sec:inverse} replaces the guess with a theorem. The observation that
does the work is that every radial metric in this setting --- circle, square,
triangle, regular $N$-gon --- is a \emph{support function}
$\rad(q)=\max_k\langle q,n_k\rangle$, and support functions come with exactly the
four constants this problem needs. They are $1$-Lipschitz, which lets us skip
indices without evaluating them, in the spirit of sphere
tracing~\cite{Hart1996Sphere}. They satisfy $\rad(q)\ge\kappa\lVert q\rVert$ with
$\kappa=\cos(\pi/N)$ exactly, which bounds how far anisotropy and rotation
together can fan the nearest index away from the radius. They are subadditive, so
the rate at which a ring's boundary outruns its own radius is not
$\lVert\delta\rVert$ but exactly $\rad(-\delta)$ --- a factor of two apart for a
triangle, and using the loose constant declares whole bands of ordinary settings
unsolvable. And when $\theta=0$ they make the index residual convex and piecewise
linear in $n$, one segment per facet, which we solve in closed form for any side
count, including a marginal case where the residual is eventually \emph{constant}
and no enumeration of any finite length can succeed.

The first three constants give a closed interval of integers that provably
contains every member passing within a stroke width of the pixel, so the minimum
over the interval is the true minimum. The interval does not involve $\theta$ at
all, which surprised us: the rotation that destroys the closed form does not widen
the search. The walk over the interval then skips most of it and calls no
transcendental function in the loop. Measured on the GPU this is
$\gpuSpeedLo$--$\gpuSpeedHi\times$ faster than the enumeration it replaces, at
equal or better fidelity, and faster than an exhaustive scan that is told in
advance where to stop.

We are also explicit about where the guarantee ends, because the boundary is
geometrically meaningful rather than merely technical. The interval is unbounded
on exactly one locus, $\rad(-\delta)=s$, and on that locus the walking family
degenerates into a \emph{tangent pencil}: every member passes through a common
point, so unboundedly many really do crowd near it. Nearby, the interval is finite
but wide. Section~\ref{sec:limits} shows what to do there, and the finding we
would most like implementors to take is that the choice of \emph{how} to subsample
matters more than how much: anchored to the family, you get a thinner but
structurally whole pattern; anchored to the pixel, you get shattered dashes at
identical cost.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A field formulation of moir\'e in which a layer is an index field plus a
  distance field, superposition is field compositing, and the pattern has no
  resolution (Section~\ref{sec:field}).

  \item The fringe law: locally averaged ink is a saturating tent in the index
  difference, so fringes are its unit level sets. Exact, per-pixel, with no
  periodicity assumption, and with a computable validity criterion --- the
  heterodyne ratio --- that can be drawn as a map
  (Sections~\ref{sec:fringe}--\ref{sec:visibility}).

  \item Walking families, and their inversion: a provably sufficient index
  interval from the support function of the generating shape, a Lipschitz skip
  rule, and an exact closed form for translated polygons of any side count
  including the marginal drift (Section~\ref{sec:inverse},
  Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).

  \item \Moire{}, an interactive tool: thirteen families, twelve concurrent
  layers compiled once, zoom-stable strokes, GPU type morphing, and no
  rasterisation step (Section~\ref{sec:system}). Its \emph{envelope} view draws
  the averaged ink directly, which requires averaging over index rather than over
  space; done properly it costs one solve and a few instructions per tap rather
  than one solve per tap (Section~\ref{sec:envelope}).

  \item Moir\'e fields as a contouring primitive: because fringes are level sets
  of an index difference, encoding a scalar field in that difference renders its
  contours without ever computing a contour. The fringes localise to
  $\contourWorstErr$\,px of the true level sets, and the only limit is a
  Nyquist-like inequality between contour spacing and carrier pitch
  (Section~\ref{sec:beyond}).

  \item An expression language for those fields, with the gradient the eikonal
  divide needs obtained by forward-mode differentiation rather than by hand, and
  compiled to straight-line shader code rather than interpreted per pixel ---
  which is what makes an arbitrary user-written field affordable at all, by
  $\fieldGainLo$--$\fieldGainHi\times$ (Section~\ref{sec:exprs}).
\end{itemize}
