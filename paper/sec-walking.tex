\section{Folds: walking families}
\label{sec:walking}

The last rung of \S\ref{sec:taxonomy} is a count with no formula at all. This
section constructs the families that live there, proves the closed-form law
that decides where they fold, and locates the exact boundary past which no
formula can invert them; the next section prices the search that remains. The
tool that makes all of it tractable is the same object the catalog's
concentric rows were quietly built from.

\subsection{Radial metrics are support functions}

Fix a convex body $K\subset\R^2$ that is symmetric under rotation by $2\pi/N$ and
contains the origin. Write
\begin{equation}
  \rad(q) \;=\; \max_{u \in U}\, \langle q, u\rangle,
  \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
where $U$ is the set of outward unit normals of $K$'s boundary. Then
$\rad(q)=R$ is precisely the boundary of the copy of $K$ with inradius $R$, so
$\rad$ is the natural ``radius'' for that shape. Equation~\eqref{eq:support} says
$\rad$ is the support function of $\mathrm{conv}(U)$~\cite{Rockafellar1970Convex}, and
the four cases our renderer exposes are all instances of it:
\begin{align}
  \text{circle:}\quad   &\rad(q) = \lVert q\rVert          &  U &= S^1, \notag\\
  \text{square:}\quad   &\rad(q) = \max(|q_x|, |q_y|)      &  U &= \{\pm e_1, \pm e_2\}, \notag\\
  \text{triangle:}\quad &\rad(q) = \max\Bigl(q_x,\; \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|q_y| - \tfrac{1}{2}q_x\Bigr), \notag\\
  \text{hexagon:}\quad  &\rad(q) = \max\Bigl(|q_x|,\; \tfrac{1}{2}|q_x| + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|q_y|\Bigr).
  \label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
The polygon forms in~\eqref{eq:metrics} are the max over $N$ unit normals written
out and folded by symmetry; for a free side count we evaluate
$\lVert q\rVert\cos(\mathrm{wrap}(\arg q, 2\pi/N))$ instead, which is the same
function. That the common cases avoid transcendentals entirely matters later:
$\rad$ is the innermost call of the solver.

We will use four properties, all immediate from~\eqref{eq:support}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(P1)] \textbf{$1$-Lipschitz.} $|\rad(a)-\rad(b)| \le \lVert a-b\rVert$,
  since $U$ lies in the unit circle. This holds across corners, where $\nabla\rad$
  jumps.
  \item[(P2)] \textbf{Anisotropy floor.}
  $\kappa\lVert q\rVert \le \rad(q) \le \lVert q\rVert$ with
  \begin{equation}
    \kappa \;=\; \min_{\lVert v\rVert=1}\rad(v) \;=\; \cos(\pi/N),
    \label{eq:kappa}
  \end{equation}
  the inradius of $\mathrm{conv}(U)$: $1$ for the circle, $1/\sqrt2$ for the
  square, $1/2$ for the triangle. Both constants in~\eqref{eq:kappa} are attained,
  so neither can be improved.
  \item[(P3)] \textbf{Homogeneity and subadditivity.} $\rad(tq)=t\rad(q)$ for
  $t\ge0$ and $\rad(a+b)\le\rad(a)+\rad(b)$. Note that $\rad$ is \emph{not}
  symmetric: $\rad(-q)\neq\rad(q)$ for the triangle. This asymmetry is exactly
  what makes the drift constant of Section~\ref{sec:drift} directional.
  \item[(P4)] \textbf{Piecewise linearity.} For a polygon, $\rad$ is a max of $N$
  linear forms, hence convex and piecewise linear with $N$ pieces.
\end{enumerate}

A \emph{walking family} is the sequence of curves
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma_n \;=\; \bigl\{\, p : \rad\bigl(\Rot{-n\theta}(p - c_n)\bigr) = ns+\varphi \,\bigr\},
  \qquad c_n = \Rot{n\theta}(n\delta),
  \label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
for $n\in\N_0$, with spacing $s>0$, phase $\varphi\ge0$ (so that every radius
$ns+\varphi$ is nonnegative), per-index translation
$\delta\in\R^2$, and per-index rotation $\theta$. Figure~\ref{fig:family} shows
one. The three degenerate cases are familiar: $\delta=0,\theta=0$ gives
concentric shapes; $\theta=0$ alone gives a family whose centers walk a straight
line; and degenerating the shape to a half-plane, whose support set $U$ is a
single normal, recovers parallel lines at spacing $s$.

Expanding $\Rot{-n\theta}(p - \Rot{n\theta}(n\delta))$ gives the pulled-back
point
\begin{equation}
  q_n(p) \;=\; \Rot{-n\theta}\,p \;-\; n\delta,
  \label{eq:qn}
\end{equation}
which is the form we compute with: a rotation of $p$ that advances by $\theta$ per
index, minus a translation that advances by $\delta$ per index. Define the
\emph{index residual}
\begin{equation}
  h_p(n) \;=\; \rad\bigl(q_n(p)\bigr) - (ns + \varphi),
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
positive when $p$ falls outside ring $n$ and negative when inside. The distance
field the renderer wants is
\begin{equation}
  d(p) \;=\; \min_{n\in\N_0} \bigl|h_p(n)\bigr|.
  \label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
This is not the Euclidean distance to $\Gamma_{n^\star}$; it is the residual of
the inradius metric, which agrees with Euclidean distance for circles and
\emph{under}estimates it for polygons, by a factor of at most $1/\kappa$:
(P1) gives residual $\le$ distance always, and the gauge comparison gives
distance $\le$ residual$/\kappa$. Renderers of this kind consume $d$ through a
smoothstep of width one pixel, so the distinction thickens apparent stroke
weight near corners and changes nothing else. We keep the metric residual
because it is what admits the bounds below; the Limitations of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} note the cost.

\subsection{The fold law}
\label{sec:foldlaw}

Whether a walking family folds at all --- and where --- does not need the
search; it is decided by the same support function, in closed form, before any
solver runs. Write $H$ for the support function of the unit body
$K=\{\rad\le1\}$, so that member $n$ --- the body of gauge radius
$\rho_n=ns+\varphi$, turned by $n\theta$, centered at $c_n$ --- has support
function
\begin{equation}
  H_n(u) \;=\; \rho_n\,H(u-n\theta)\;+\;\langle c_n,\hat u\rangle,
  \qquad \hat u=(\cos u,\sin u).
  \label{eq:famsupport}
\end{equation}
Treat the index as continuous for a moment. The derivative
$\partial H_n/\partial n$ is the \emph{normal speed} of the moving front at
contact angle $u$: how fast the member's supporting line in direction $\hat u$
advances per index.

\begin{lemma}[Fold law]
\label{lem:foldlaw}
The boundary-of-member map $(u,n)\mapsto H_n(u)\,\hat u+H_n'(u)\,\hat u^{\perp}$
has Jacobian determinant $-(H_n+H_n'')\,\partial_n H_n$, and $H_n+H_n''\ge0$
for a convex member. The family therefore folds --- neighboring members
touch, and an envelope forms --- exactly where the normal speed vanishes:
\begin{equation}
  s\,H(w)\;-\;\rho_n\,\theta\,H'(w)\;+\;\langle c'(n),\hat u\rangle \;=\; 0,
  \qquad w=u-n\theta.
  \label{eq:foldlaw}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:foldlaw}; for a polygon, whose $H$ has
corners, the statement holds piecewise, the suprema below are attained at
the corners, and the corners contribute an envelope component of their own
(the vertex trajectories, which Figure~\ref{fig:foldlaw} draws). In the
terms of \S\ref{sec:taxonomy}, \eqref{eq:foldlaw} \emph{is} the discriminant
of the incidence projection, computed: $\partial_\nu$ of a front's defining
equation is its normal speed. What the lemma buys is that the fate of every
family in this section can be read off it by inspection.

\begin{corollary}[Rotation folds everything but circles]
\label{cor:rotation}
With $\delta=0$ and $\theta\neq0$, the family folds at gauge radius
\begin{equation}
  \rho^\star \;=\; \frac{s}{\theta\,L},
  \qquad L=\sup_w\,\lvert H'(w)/H(w)\rvert,
  \label{eq:foldradius}
\end{equation}
and nowhere before. For the regular $k$-gon $L=\tan(\pi/k)$, and the first
crossings appear at the vertices, at Euclidean radius
$R^\star=s/(\theta\sin(\pi/k))$. For an ellipse gauge with semi-axes $a\ge b$,
$L=(a^2-b^2)/2ab$ and $R^\star=\rho^\star\sqrt{(a^2+b^2)/2}$. For the circle
$L=0$: the circle is the unique gauge whose family survives rotation at every
radius, so the dispatch rule ``rotating a radial metric is a no-op''
(\S\ref{sec:closed}) is a theorem about circles, not an accident of
implementation.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[The Mach condition]
\label{cor:mach}
With $\theta=0$, the family folds if and only if $\rad(-\delta)>s$: the center
outruns the growth. The drift constant $m=\rad(-\delta)$, which
\S\ref{sec:drift} will derive as a search bound and
Proposition~\ref{prop:closed} recovers as an asymptotic slope, is a
\emph{Mach number}: a subsonic family ($m<s$) nests forever, a supersonic one
folds from its first member along straight wake lines (for circles, the
tangent pair $\langle p,\nu\rangle=\varphi$ with
$\langle\delta,\nu\rangle=-s$), and the marginal drift $m=s$ --- the locus
where the interval of Theorem~\ref{thm:window} degenerates and the residual
of Figure~\ref{fig:convex} goes flat --- is the sonic boundary. With rotation
and translation together, circles have
$\lVert c'(n)\rVert=\lVert\delta\rVert\sqrt{1+n^2\theta^2}$, so every rotating
walk eventually goes supersonic, at
$n^\star=\sqrt{(s/\lVert\delta\rVert)^2-1}\,/\,\theta$.
\end{corollary}

Figure~\ref{fig:foldlaw} is the law drawn over renders that never saw it. One
consequence worth stating plainly: the integrability ladder of
\S\ref{sec:taxonomy} is not an empirical sorting. Whether a walking family
stays a drawing (exact), and where it tears (fold), is itself a closed-form
computation --- Eq.~\eqref{eq:foldlaw} --- made before any pixel is solved.

\subsection{The inversion problem}
\label{sec:inversion}

Equation~\eqref{eq:field} is a minimization over an infinite discrete set, so
every practical solver is an argument about which finitely many $n$ can be
skipped. Where the argument can be a formula turns out to have an exact
boundary, and it is arithmetic.

Below the rotation rung the whole problem is convex: with $\theta=0$,
$h_p(n)=\rad(p-n\delta)-ns-\varphi$ is a convex gauge evaluated along an
affine path, hence convex in $n$ for \emph{every} shape. The classical cases
are one fact seen three times. $\delta=0$ makes $h_p$ affine, so the answer is
one \texttt{mod}; a circle gauge makes it a conditioned quadratic, so two
integer evaluations suffice; a polygon gauge makes it piecewise linear, and
Section~\ref{sec:closed} solves that case exactly, marginal drift included
(we have not found it stated elsewhere). Convexity also extends the catalog
sideways at no cost: an ellipse gauge under translation is the circle's
quadratic with an inner product swapped in --- verified exact against brute
force in \texttt{tools\pslash exp\pslash foldlaw.mjs} --- so an aspect ratio
on the concentric families would inherit the solver unchanged. (The
constants (P1)--(P2) rescale by the eccentricity; convexity and the
quadratic do not care.)

Rotation is different, and the boundary of the formulas is arithmetic:

\begin{proposition}[Rational twists untwist]
\label{prop:untwist}
If $\theta=2\pi a/b$ in lowest terms, the walking family splits into $b$
translated subfamilies: on the class $n\equiv j\pmod b$ the frame angle is
the constant $j\theta$, the centers walk the fixed direction
$\Rot{j\theta}\delta$, and $h_p$ restricted to the class is convex in $n$, so
the inverse is $b$ closed forms (for circles, $b$ quadratics; verified exact
against brute force at $\theta=4\pi/7$). That is the \emph{only} mechanism a
formula can use: the set of frames $\{n\theta \bmod 2\pi\}$ is finite exactly
when $\theta/2\pi$ is rational, and no formula absorbs the frame continuously
instead, because for any $\theta\neq0$ the incidence relation of the
interpolated family, $\{(p,\nu):h_p(\nu)=0\}$, is not semialgebraic: a
semialgebraic incidence would force
$\nu\mapsto\langle c(\nu),e_1\rangle=\nu\lVert\delta\rVert\cos(\nu\theta+\beta)$
to be algebraic over $\R(\nu)$, and it is transcendental
(Appendix~\ref{app:foldlaw}, stated for the circle gauge).
\end{proposition}

So the search this section sets up is not a concession to a hard integral;
for a generic twist there is provably no equation to solve. The residual
mixes its unknown polynomially and trigonometrically ---
$\lVert p-c(n)\rVert^2=(ns+\varphi)^2$ carries a term in
$n\cos(n\theta+\beta)$ --- which is the class of Kepler's equation, solved by
guarded iteration for three centuries because there is no formula to
write~\cite{Colwell1993Kepler}. Our case is quantifiably worse than Kepler's,
whose inverse is at least single-valued: past the fold radius of
Corollary~\ref{cor:rotation} the number of members of the interpolated family
through a point grows linearly with radius (the gate counts
$\foldBranchCounts$ members at radii $200$ through $3200$), where an
algebraic relation of degree $D$ admits at most $D$ anywhere. The question is
therefore not how to solve $h_p(n)=0$, but how few integers we can prove it
suffices to test.

The naive answer is $n \approx \rad(p)/s$, ``the ring whose radius matches the
pixel's radius,'' plus a fixed number of neighbors. The next section shows how
far that can be from the truth, and replaces it with a provably sufficient
interval.
