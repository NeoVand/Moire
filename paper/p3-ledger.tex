\subsection{What is classical}
None of the following is ours, and the paper leans on all of it. That two
sinusoids beat and that a nonlinearity demodulates the beat is
Helmholtz~\cite{Helmholtz1954Sensations}, and every radio detector since.
That the bands of two gratings are the level lines of a difference of
counts is the indicial picture of the moir\'e
literature~\cite{Oster1964, Amidror2009Theory}, which also has a complete
Fourier theory of which orders exist for periodic gratings. That an
aperiodic family has a count is de Bruijn's index function for
multigrids~\cite{deBruijn1981Penrose}, and the torus of an aperiodic
superposition is the hull of the physics of incommensurate
solids~\cite{Bellissard2000Hulls}. That averaging along a subgroup is a
conditional expectation is ergodic theory's, and that convergents are the
best approximations and the golden ratio the worst approximable number is
Lagrange's, Hurwitz's and Khinchin's~\cite{Hurwitz1891Annaherung,
Khinchin1964Continued}. Defects with quantized charge are the dislocations
of singular optics~\cite{NyeBerry1974Dislocations}.

\subsection{What is ours}
The claim of the paper is that these are one subject, and that the one
object $S=I\circ\Phi$ makes them so: every phenomenon above is a property
of the state map, of the picture, or of the way an observer averages one
over the other. The specific contributions are: the sharp conditional
expectation for whole-number slides (Theorem~\ref{thm:sweep}) and the soft
one for windows with its explicit leakage (Theorem~\ref{thm:multiplier});
the observer theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:universality}) with its corollaries
--- who can see a beat, hard patterns observer-proof, soft ones not ---
and the universal-invariant proposition that makes ``the third pattern''
a quotient (Proposition~\ref{prop:invariant}); selection as best
approximation with the harmonic price, so that stations are places on a
pair whose ratio varies, and the threshold on the complete quotient with
its deserts (\S\ref{sec:selection}); the duty null
(Theorem~\ref{thm:dutynull}); the cascade theorem for beats of beats
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cascade}); the trichotomy of counts with the fold law
and the defect count (\S\ref{sec:counting}); and an instrument in which
every one of these is a measurement (\S\ref{sec:instrument}).

\subsection{How sure}
Of the theorems: certain; they are proved above, and the proofs are short
enough to check. Of the drawn predictions: certain; they are measured
through the shipped renderer against brute-force truth, and the table is
their record. Of the predictions in sound: the arithmetic is certain and
the physics adds assumptions --- that an ear applies a bending front end
before it pools, that a two-stage system exists in it --- which are
standard but not ours, and the ear of Figure~\ref{fig:ear} is a
\emph{model} of an ear, four lines of code, not a person. What the theory
does not yet have is a prediction that a physicist would find surprising
and that has been tested outside the tool on something that is not a
simulation. The next subsection is the list of what would change that, in
order of how little it would cost.

\subsection{Where the depth is, and is not}
The mathematics is elementary: every theorem is a few lines from Fourier
series, the implicit function theorem and the arithmetic of convergents,
and a good student can prove each in an afternoon once the statement is in
front of them. That is a strength for a theory meant to be understood and
a weakness for one meant to impress. The depth is in the unification. The
same theorem says why a mistuned octave of square waves does not beat, why
a wheel with $50\%$ sectors cannot show the $2{:}1$ station under a strobe,
why a blurred grating reopens a null under a squaring observer while a
sharp one does not, and why a symmetric pair of coupled oscillators has no
even locking tongue --- that last one a known symmetry fact of dynamics,
unified here rather than discovered. A theory that answers questions it
was not built to answer is the standard we are trying to meet; the duty
null and the temporal selection were the first two such answers, neither
sought, both measured within the hour of being derived.

\subsection{What to test at home, and what it would show}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The octave null} (Try-it, \S\ref{sec:whosees}). Two pulse
waves at $200$ and $405$ hertz; the five-hertz beat present at $30\%$ duty,
absent at $50\%$, present at $70\%$. Cheap; a synthesizer or a web page. It
would confirm Theorem~\ref{thm:dutynull} in a real ear.
\item \emph{The order of the ear's front end.} The same, with the pulses'
edges softened by a low-pass on each oscillator. If the null at $50\%$
survives, the ear's effective front end is quadratic; if the beat comes
back, it is of higher order (Corollary~\ref{cor:soft}). Cheap, and not in
the literature in this form as far as we can find.
\item \emph{Beats of beats} (Try-it, \S\ref{sec:iterated}). Three sines at
$300$, $330$, $363$ hertz; a three-hertz throb needs two stages of
demodulation. The psychoacoustics of ``second-order beats'' has data; the
theorem says what the data must show.
\item \emph{The desert.} Two sawtooth tones at the golden ratio against two
at $2.05$: the second beats, the first is rough and does not.
\item \emph{The wagon wheel.} A spoked wheel under a strobe, or a fan on a
phone's video: the theory says the perceived rotation is the priced
convergent of the spoke rate over the frame rate, and that its reversals
sit at the convergents. Not yet scripted; an afternoon.
\item \emph{The long exposure} (Try-it, \S\ref{sec:pooling}). Two window
screens, one moving, a phone in night mode: the bands survive and the
members do not, and moving both at rates $1{:}2$ keeps a different set of
bands.
\end{enumerate}
