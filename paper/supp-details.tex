\section{Envelope and solver details}
\label{supp:details}

Two implementation notes referenced from the main text: how each family spends
the envelope's advancing index, and how to read the two below-unity rows of the
ablation.

\subsection{Every family has a currency}

The envelope view advances the ranked layers at the integer rates that hold the
criterion's winning character fixed (\S\ref{sec:visibility}) and averages over
$u\in[0,1)$: the plain diagonal for a difference beat, $(1,-1)$ for a sum beat,
and $(\lvert k_2\rvert,\mp k_1)$ in general -- $(1,2)$ for a $(2,-1)$ beat,
$(1,3)$ for the $3{:}1$ pair's $(3,-1)$, any order the per-pixel reduction of
\S\ref{sec:selection} certifies -- every other layer riding the diagonal. A
rate of $q$ samples each period $M/q$ times at $M$ taps, so deep convergents
want the tap dial raised. On $K$ layers the diagonal alone preserves the zero-sum
sublattice, rank $K-1$, so the only per-pixel decision is whether a
non-zero-sum character wins and which pair spends the deviation; the scan
compares all pairs among the three topmost scalar families together with the
zero-sum ternaries, because a deviation granted to the top pair on the
strength of a two-layer scan can outrank, and then scramble, a slower beat
against the third. (To isolate a single character $k$ on $K$ layers in
general, sweep along $w_1+\gamma w_2$ with $w_1,w_2$ integer vectors spanning
a complement of $k$ in $k^\perp\cap\Z^K$: an integer character survives the
average iff it is orthogonal to both, i.e.\ iff it is a multiple of $k$. The
two-layer rule and the twist pair's $w=(1,\gamma,1,\gamma)$ are the rank-one
and rank-two instances.) Each family realizes its advance in its own
parameter. The
level-set families shift a phase (a rate of $2$ advances up to a full local
period, so the slide offset wraps back into the half-period the solved trio
brackets; exact for phase families, whose residual is periodic in the gap).
The radial pencil turns: advancing its index by $u$ rotates the fan by
$u\pi/N$, which is how a family whose index is an angle joins an average built
for scalars.

A lattice indexes its members by a \emph{pair} of integers, so its phase lives
on a two-torus and the diagonal of \S\ref{sec:envelope} is not a point of bookkeeping but a
choice of direction. Two wrong answers bracket the right one. Averaging over the
whole torus washes the lattice to its mean coverage, and with it every fringe
it makes against the rest of the stack, since a one-dimensional correlation is
measure zero there. Stepping both generators in lockstep keeps the lattice's own
indices correlated, and it then beats against itself: a fine hatch at the
carrier's scale that no contrast can be read through.

The right answer is a rank-1 slice of the torus, oriented per point. Which of a
lattice's index combinations carries the visible beat is the character choice
of \S\ref{sec:visibility} again, here over integer combinations of the generators. The candidate
set must follow the ink, and the honeycomb taught us how far that reaches: its
hexagon walls of one orientation repeat at pitch $(\sqrt3/2)s$, and the dual
vectors normal to the three wall families are $(1,-1)$, $(2,1)$, and
$(1,2)$: the \emph{second} ring of the dual lattice, not the first. An
enumeration that stops at the generators, their sum, and their difference
covers one wall family in three; against a ring partner the other two sectors
keep their fringes in the render and lose them in the view. Nor does the ink
stop there: the third ring is the \emph{doubled} generators $(2,0)$, $(0,2)$,
$(2,2)$ (the honeycomb's vertex rows repeat at half the row pitch, and a
thin-lined grid is rich in each family's second harmonic), and against the
same ring partner the ring-3 beat is nearly as slow as the wall beat and owns
the sectors thirty degrees away. So the candidates are the first three dual
rings, each counted either way, and each weighted by where its kind puts
ink: the honeycomb's fundamental is its second ring, the square grid has
nothing at $(2,1)$ or $(2,2)$ at all, and the weight multiplies the match
error so a near-tie resolves toward the family that carries contrast. (This
enumeration is still finite; higher coincidence rings exist and sit outside
the schedule -- unlike the scalar characters, whose scan is order-free by the
reduction of \S\ref{sec:selection}.)
Every combination is linear in position, so its index gradient is exact, and the
view holds still, per pixel, the one whose gradient best matches the ranked
partner's, forward or backward. The complementary direction runs a golden-ratio
rule, $\{i\varphi\}$ at the $i$th tap: the Fibonacci lattice, a rank-1 rule
on the two-torus~\cite{Sloan1994Lattice, Keller2013Quasi}. Each schedule is the
unimodular completion that sends its own combination to the sweep $u$ and every
other candidate to a nonzero mix of $u$ and $\{i\varphi\}$: a generator rides
$(u,\,\{i\varphi\})$, the sum rides $(u-\{i\varphi\},\,\{i\varphi\})$, the
difference $(\{i\varphi\},\,\{i\varphi\}-u)$, and the second-ring pairs
$(u-\{i\varphi\},\, 2\{i\varphi\}-u)$ and $(u-2\{i\varphi\},\, \{i\varphi\})$.
The doubled combinations have no unimodular completion: holding $(2,0)$
puts the bare generator on $u/2$, which averages that one carrier over only
half a period; the residue sits at carrier scale beneath a coarse fringe and
is accepted. A self-beat would need two combinations held at once, which no
schedule provides.

Two lattices with no scalar layer between them are the one case the partner
gradient cannot orient, and the beat there is \emph{rank two}: a small twist
between equal lattices leaves both matched-generator differences slow at once,
and the moir\'e is itself a lattice. The right sweep is again the one
orthogonal to every kept character, here $w=(1,\gamma,1,\gamma)$ on the
four-torus: the topmost lattice rides $(u,\{i\gamma\})$ directly, and every
other lattice matches each of its generators, signed, to the reference's per
pixel and rides the \emph{same} schedule. Matched generators advance in
lockstep across layers, so both slow differences survive every tap exactly,
while each carrier, each self-beat, and every cross combination rides a nonzero
mix of $u$ and $\gamma$ and averages away. Without this the orientation choice
degenerates to a per-pixel tie-break on derivative noise, and the envelope of
an ordinary twist pair drowns in speckle.

We record the choice because our first implementation fixed it globally (one
generator, one way) and the tool exposed the error: a partner family's counting
direction rotates around its center, so whole sectors of a superposition kept
their fringes in the render and lost them in the view. \S\ref{sec:visibility}'s minimum is not a
nicety. An average that fixes the convention globally deletes the moir\'es that
live in the other gauge.

\subsection{A pivot, not a gain}

The view exposes a contrast, because a fringe field is a small excursion about
the stack's mean coverage and the interesting part is the excursion. Expanding
about $0$ would ride the mean up and down as the fringes move, so the expansion
is about the coverage the stack would average over its own periods: $2h/s$ per
family, composited. Counting a lattice's families is not optional. A hex lattice
draws three, and calling its coverage that of one puts the pivot far brighter
than the picture it is expanding about, which at any real contrast drives the
whole frame black; that is how we found the bug.

\subsection{Reading the ablation's negative rows}

Removing the carried rotation or the accept-early-exit makes the three scanning
ablation scenes $\ablCostLo$--$\ablCostHi\%$ \emph{faster}. Removing the accept exit is faster because in a
heavily inked region almost every lane would exit anyway, and the branch only
adds divergence: with $32$ lanes in lockstep, one slow lane sets the pace, so an
early exit that helps $31$ of them helps nobody. Both mechanisms stay because
they are what make sparse and mid-density scenes cheap, and because a solver
tuned only for saturated fields is tuned for the case where the image carries
least information. The same wavefront-divergence argument says why narrowing the
interval, rather than exiting the loop sooner, is the productive lever in this
problem: the interval is computed per pixel before the loop and shrinks the work
of \emph{every} lane, while an exit condition only helps whichever lane is
already done.
