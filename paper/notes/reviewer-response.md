# Response to the review of the snapshot

Thank you. This is the most useful reading the work has had. I agree with each of the four mathematical corrections, and with the framing you propose: the contribution is a representation that keeps joint phases, harmonic cancellations and curvature alive through a shader until the filter can act on their combined effect, and the paper should be built around that and measured as that. I checked every number you gave, found each correct, and made the corrections in the text and, where they touch code, in the compiler with a gate, before writing this. Below I take the points in order, say what the corrected statement is and what the compiler actually did (which was worse than the snapshot claimed in one case and better in none), and end with the questions I would like your view on.

## 1. The priced merit is a search heuristic, not a visibility law

You are right, and the counterexample settles it: for $x = \sqrt2$, $\mu_{1,1} = 0.343146$ and $\mu_{3,2} = 0.353247$, so the convergent $3/2$ sets no record, and along the convergents of $\sqrt2$ the merit rises towards its limit $1/(2\sqrt2)$ instead of falling. The proposition as printed claimed both directions and the "every convergent" direction is false.

I also read what the script's gate had tested. It tested the plain slowness $|h - kx|$, the record setters of which are the convergents by Lagrange, and reported that as if it were a statement about $\mu$. It was not. I have now measured the priced statement itself, by denominator (for each $k$ the best $h$; a record beats every smaller $k$), over the same $207$ ratios with $k \le 300$: every record of $\mu$ is a convergent ($452$ of $452$), and most convergents are not records ($452$ of $1{,}246$; $205$ of the $207$ ratios have a convergent that sets no record). So what survives is one-directional, and it survives for the reason you point to: $\mu$ is $k|kx - h|$ up to a factor near one, so a record of $\mu$ is close to a best approximation of the second kind.

The corrected text says three things. The visible contribution of a recipe is $|\widehat{N\circ I}(k)|\,|\widehat W(\rho\nu_k)|$ and nothing else; a vanishing coefficient overrides any geometry, which is what the duty-null theorem already said, so the two statements were in tension on the same page. The price $|ab|$ stands in for the coefficient under the generic assumption $|\hat g(n)| \sim 1/n$, and only under it does least $\mu$ mean strongest. The continued fraction is the candidate ladder: the lattice reduction proposes recipes, and each is scored by its actual coefficient and multiplier, which is what the compiler does and the only thing it does. The station identity $\mu_n = 2h_nk_n/((h_n+k_nx)(x_{n+1}k_n+k_{n-1}))$ and the threshold on the complete quotient stay as identities about the merit, relabelled as geometric stations that show for generic profiles and are extinguished by nulls.

## 2. Two-valued pictures: affine in coverage, not commuting

Agreed. The identity is $\mathbb E[N(I)] = N(0) + (N(1)-N(0))\,\mathbb E[I]$: the filtered nonlinear result is an affine function of the coverage, which is why coverage suffices, and it is not commutation ($\mathbb E[I^2] = \tfrac12 \ne \tfrac14$ at half coverage). The theorem three sections earlier had it right and the anti-aliasing table had it wrong; the table now states the identity and the counterexample.

## 3. The scope of "exact"

Three separate things, and the text had run them together.

- The whole-number slide is an orthogonal projection. That statement is exact and stays.
- The Gaussian blur is the multiplier sum with explicit leakage $|\widehat W(\rho\nu_k)|$ on every recipe. "The blur reports $\mathbb E[I\mid\text{slow}]$" is a limit statement, as the fast rates go to infinity relative to $1/\rho$, and a small heterodyne ratio does not by itself give that separation, since $\eta$ compares a recipe's rate to the carrier's and not to the window's. The text now says limit, and the universality theorem says limit.
- The remainder hypothesis $\sum_k |k||\hat I(k)| < \infty$ excludes hard profiles. Two repairs, and the compiler already uses the second. Keeping the oscillatory factor the proof bounds by one weights the remainder by the multiplier, $\sum_k |k||\hat I(k)|\,|\widehat W(\rho\nu_k)|$, which converges for hard profiles wherever the recipes' rates are fast, the window supplying the decay; that is the far-field case. Where recipes are slow the series does not decay and the compiler does not use it: it integrates such counts by quadrature with located jumps, whose error is a quadrature error and not a series remainder. So what the compiler guarantees is a per-pixel budget: the second-order model of the counts (the third-order phase is the floor), the retained-recipe cut, and the quadrature tolerances. The text now says budget wherever it said exact, and it no longer uses the word for the frame-level comparison, which, as you say, only shows that the reference cannot resolve more.

## 4. The stationary-count rule needs curvature

Your counterexample is correct and the compiler failed it. With $\xi = At^2$, $p = \cos 2\pi u$, $A = 1$, $\sigma = \tfrac12$, the exact pixel is $\mathrm{Re}\,(1 - 4\pi i A\sigma^2)^{-1/2} = 0.4446$, and the compiler returned $1$: its freeze rule fired on any count with rate below $10^{-9}$ and substituted the centre value. A thousandth of a pixel off the stationary point the rate was nonzero, the axis went to the local path, and the answer was $0.4449$, wrong by $3.5\times10^{-4}$, because the local path's line quadrature also modelled the count as linear. From a tenth of a pixel off the axis was spectral and the answer exact, because for spectral axes the Hessian enters every recipe's phase and the quadratic-phase integral is closed form. So the machinery was there and the decision ignored it.

The correction is the one you describe: the width of a count under the pixel is now the standard deviation of $g\cdot z + \tfrac12 z^THz$ for $z \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I)$, which is $\sqrt{\sigma^2|g|^2 + \tfrac12\sigma^4\|H\|_F^2}$, and that width decides. An axis is frozen only when the width is under $10^{-9}$, or when it is narrow (under the local threshold) and mostly curvature, and then it is held at the count's mean over the pixel, $u_0 + \tfrac12\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}H$, rather than its centre value. A narrow axis whose rate dominates is local, sized by its first-order width, which is what the line quadrature models. Everything else is spectral, including a stationary count wide from its curvature, which is your example. A gate now holds the compiler to the closed form at three curvatures and five offsets (all agree to $10^{-10}$) and to a two-million-sample reference for the hard-edged version, the sign of the cosine, where it is within $2\times10^{-5}$ at the stationary pixel and $3\times10^{-3}$ half a pixel off, the far harmonics decaying only as $k^{-3/2}$ under the cut.

Two things I learned making the change. The benchmark contained an instance: the fire shader's count $0.005x^2$ is stationary on the axis $x = 240$, and holding it at the pixel mean instead of the centre took that pixel from $2.8\times10^{-4}$ to $1.3\times10^{-4}$. And a narrow curvature-dominated count must be frozen at its mean rather than made spectral: made spectral, its harmonics are not suppressed at all (a stationary count suppresses nothing), so the palette field that rides on it has to be summed over its whole torus coordinate, and under the harmonic cap that cost $3\times10^{-3}$. Every other probe pixel of the benchmark is unchanged.

## On novelty

I take the point. Yang and Barnes 2018 has Gaussian smoothing of programs, automatic rule selection, and a statistical treatment of dependencies; the distinction is not "Gaussian expectation" or "automatic". It is what the intermediate representation keeps: the joint phase of every count (so that two fast inputs whose combination is slow are never averaged separately), harmonic ranges sized for cancellation between counts (the stations), the curvature of each count (the second-order pushforward), and located jumps in the near field. The rippled shaders show it most concretely, because the parallax couples the pattern's counts to the ripple's and the surviving recipes are combinations no per-node moment can see. I will make that the paper's claim and measure it as such, with the ablations you describe.

## The held-out experiment

I will run it as you specify, and it is cheap for us because the same harness traces a shader from source: a frozen compiler with no threshold changed, a continuously varying family (pitch ratio, duty, curvature of the count, phase, camera scale, and a second geometry), mathematically equivalent source expressions of one shader ($\mathrm{fract}(u) - \tfrac12$ against $u - \lfloor u \rfloor - \tfrac12$, a sine against its cosine identity, a select against its arithmetic), unclamped and unquantised error against $10^6$ to $10^7$ Gaussian samples at probe pixels, worst cases, temporal stability under a moving camera, and total cost including table construction. Ablations: harmonic ranges sized per axis alone instead of for cancellation; first-order pushforward only; fields as numbers instead of jets; no located jumps; first-order widths instead of curvature-aware ones (this last knob exists already). Each of these was once the state of the compiler and each cost a wrong pixel, so I expect them to be sharp, but the point is to show it on shaders the compiler has not seen.

## On speed, and the control variate

I agree that the GPU is not an argument. Seconds to minutes a pixel where the view enters a transform means the work must be reused or avoided, and a fast implementation of an expensive algorithm is an expensive implementation. The honest role of the current compiler is the one you name: a reference that finds failures and measures approximations.

The control-variate direction is the one I would most like to pursue, and your point about variance is the part I had not seen. With $q$ the analytically integrable part (the retained recipes with their exact Gaussian means) and $\widehat C = \mathbb E[q] + \tfrac1n\sum_j (S(z_j) - q(z_j))$, the estimator is unbiased for any fixed $q$, so every approximation in the model is corrected by the residual and the compiler's job becomes choosing $q$. A fast carrier with near-zero filtered mean can dominate the variance of the residual, so it belongs in $q$ even though a mean-oriented pruning discards it; that gives the theory a second selection criterion, variance removed per unit cost, beside visibility. The multiplier gives the mean of a recipe and the coefficient its variance contribution ($\tfrac12|\hat I(k)|^2$ for a wave with a fast rate), so both criteria are available before any sample is taken. This also settles coverage: the structures the compiler cannot trace (fire with bumps today) fall into the residual and are sampled, at a cost the variance criterion predicts. I will build it into the harness as a mode and measure it on the same held-out family: error against samples for plain supersampling, for the current hybrid (exact where cheap, samples elsewhere), and for the control variate with mean-selected and with variance-selected $q$.

## The horizon row, honestly

The row where the perspective's rates change fourfold across the pixel is outside the second-order model, and the remedy I had running when your review arrived does not work. Treating the pixel as a Gauss–Hermite mixture of twenty-five narrower Gaussians ($\sigma = 0.25$), each traced and expanded at its own centre, gives $5\times10^{-3}$ at the circles' horizon pixel where the plain pixel gives $1.5\times10^{-3}$, although each sub-pixel on its own is within $3\times10^{-4}$ to $6\times10^{-4}$ of a million samples. The sub-pixel value varies with the sub-pixel's centre on the pattern's own scale, and five nodes cannot integrate that. I also checked that the curvature growth cap of the harmonic ranges is not the cause: the horizon pixels are unchanged with the cap raised from $2$ to $8$. What remains is a third-order pushforward (the Gaussian integral of a cubic phase, which is an Airy-type function) or a one-dimensional quadrature along the direction of the third derivative. Until one of those is built, the row is a stated limit and not a cost.

## Questions

1. **What would establish the representation claim to your satisfaction?** The ablations on the held-out family, or a direct comparison of intermediate representations, running the published mean-variance code on the same held-out shaders and reporting where it fails and where we fail? I can do both; the second needs their code to run on arbitrary source, which I have not checked.
2. **What standard for "exact" would you accept in print?** I propose reporting, per pixel, the three terms of the budget (second-order remainder bound, retained-recipe cut, quadrature tolerance) beside the value, and verifying at selected pixels against $10^7$ unclamped samples. Is a stated budget with spot verification enough, or would you want the remainder bound proved for hard profiles with the multiplier weight before the word is used at all?
3. **On the held-out family:** would a random grammar over the primitives (random compositions with random constants, filtered for non-degeneracy) be more convincing as "unseen" than parametric sweeps of the published shaders, or less, because random programs are not what people write? My inclination is both, with the random set small and reported in full.
4. **On the control variate:** do you know a published criterion for choosing analytic terms by variance per cost in a residual-sampling estimator? The primary-space work you point to constructs $q$ from samples; ours would construct it from the program's spectrum before any sample, and I would like to cite the closest prior statement of the selection problem.
5. **On the third-order row:** is there a standard form you would point me to for the Gaussian expectation of $e^{i(b z + \tfrac12 q z^2 + \tfrac16 c z^3)}$ beyond the Airy function in one dimension, or would you accept a one-dimensional quadrature along the third derivative's direction, with the other direction closed form, as the exact-to-tolerance treatment?
6. **On the priced merit:** would you accept the continued-fraction machinery restated as a candidate generator (the lattice reduction proposes recipes; each is scored by $|\hat I(k)||\widehat W(\rho\nu_k)|$), with the record statement kept in its one-directional measured form and the station threshold labelled generic-profile?
7. **Scope of the paper:** would you keep the moiré theory (stations, deserts, duty nulls) in the anti-aliasing paper as the origin of the representation, or cut it to a paragraph and cite it separately? Your closing paragraph suggests the latter and I lean that way, but the duty null is the cleanest demonstration that phase and coefficient must be carried together, which is the paper's thesis.
