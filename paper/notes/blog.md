# The moiré is the derivative

*Notes toward a blog post about the Moiré Fields paper. Two pages, no equations that don't earn their place.*

Draw a comb of parallel lines on tracing paper. Draw a second comb, lay it over the first, and turn it two degrees. A third pattern appears: broad soft bands that sweep across the page as you turn, made of nothing but the ink already there. The bands are not on either sheet. You can chase them with a pencil and there is nothing to trace. So where are they?

The paper's answer is that the bands live in a quantity nobody was writing down. Give every line in a family a number: this is line 40, this is line 41. Now extend that numbering off the lines, so that a point a third of the way between them is 40.333. That function, the *index field*, is defined at every point of the plane, and it is the family's own private coordinate. A second family carries a second coordinate. Where the two coordinates agree modulo one, ink lands on ink and the page stays light; where they disagree by exactly half, the strokes interleave and the page reads dark. The bands you cannot trace are the level sets of the difference of the two numbering systems. A moiré is a contour plot of a disagreement.

That sounds like a metaphor. The paper makes it a theorem, and the theorem has more in it than the slogan. Average the ink over one local period of the pattern, which is what your eye does when the lines are too fine to resolve, and the result depends on position only through the index difference. The profile is not a sine wave. It is a tent with a flat top: most of the fringe sits at full contrast, and the transition occupies a sliver whose width is the sum of the stroke widths. That is why moiré looks crisp, and why thickening your pen past a point stops changing the fringe and only darkens the page. None of this needs the patterns to be periodic, or straight, or even to have a formula. It needs the two numbering systems to disagree slowly compared with the pattern itself, and that condition is itself a number you can compute at every pixel and draw as a map: bright here means no fringe will form here, before you draw a thing.

There is one honest subtlety. A numbering is a convention. Relabel the lines and the difference changes, so which combination of the two indices is "the" moiré? All of them are, at once. Every integer combination of the two coordinates is a candidate beat, and the visible pattern is whichever combination varies slowest. Turn the second comb until the gradients oppose and it is the *sum*, not the difference, that goes slow. Detune the pitches to a 2:1 ratio and a second-order combination wins. The right picture is that a stack of K patterns maps each point of the plane onto a K-dimensional torus, the ink is a fixed function on that torus, and the beats are the characters of the torus pulled back to the plane. The slow characters are the visible ones. Physicists will recognize this instantly: it is the same bookkeeping that turns two graphene sheets twisted by 1.1 degrees into a superlattice with its own electronic life. Your eye has been doing condensed-matter physics on wallpaper all along.

Once fringes are level sets of a difference, you can run the arrow backward, and this is my favorite part. Take one comb. Make the second comb the same comb, displaced by a scalar field of your choosing: at each point, shift the phase by the field's value there. The index difference is now your field, exactly, and the fringes are its contours. No marching squares, no cell tables, no ambiguous saddles, no polylines. The contour is never computed; it is a place where two strokes happen to coincide, drawn by the same two distance lookups that drew the strokes. The paper measures how well the bright band centers land on the true mathematical contours: a fraction of a pixel. The oldest measuring instruments in the book, shadow moiré and moiré interferometry, are this construction run by physics instead of by a shader, and the paper's version composes with anything you can type into an expression box, live, at any zoom.

The second half of the paper is the price of admission, and it is a genuinely different kind of work. The families artists actually reach for are the ones where each ring carries its own displacement and its own rotation, so the nest shears and spirals. Two extra sliders, and the closed-form index is gone: no formula in the plane has those curves as level sets, and "which ring is nearest to this pixel" becomes a search over the integers. The naive answer, guess from the radius and check a few neighbors, fails in the worst possible way. The failures are not speckle; they are whole arcs of whole rings, deleted coherently, because the guess fails coherently. The fix is an old piece of convex geometry. The "radius" of a square or a hexagon is a support function, and support functions hand you exactly four things: a Lipschitz bound that lets you skip rings provably too far to matter, a floor on how anisotropic the metric can be, a drift rate that is tighter than the obvious one by up to a factor of two, and, when the rings translate without rotating, outright convexity of the residual. Out of those four facts comes a closed interval of integers that provably contains every ring that could touch the pixel, and the whole thing runs ten to thirty-five times faster than the enumeration it replaced while agreeing with brute force bit for bit.

Buried in that section is my favorite fact in the paper. When the per-ring drift exactly equals the ring spacing, the residual goes flat: infinitely many rings are *exactly equidistant* from a generic point. No enumeration of any length can finish, because the answer is not at the end of the list; the list has no end. But convexity reads the flat segment off in one comparison. There is something satisfying about a case that is unsolvable by patience and trivial by structure.

What stays with me is the shape of the whole project. Nothing here is a new algorithm in the machine-learning sense; it is a change of representation, from marks to coordinates, and every good thing follows from naming the right quantity. The fringe law is the difference of two names. The validity map is the ratio of two gradients. Four of the tool's shipped bugs were found because a named quantity carried units and the units refused to agree. The paper reads like an argument that the moiré was never really about ink at all. The ink is a carrier. The pattern is the derivative of a disagreement, and once you write the disagreement down, you own the pattern.
