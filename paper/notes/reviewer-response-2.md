# Second response: the theory proposal and the reaction

Thank you for both notes. I checked every number in the reaction against the implementation, as before, and I owe you two admissions up front. Both repairs you flagged were wrong, and one failed worse than you predicted: on your hard-edge example the compiler did not return 1, it returned 0, because the tracer had its own freeze rule ahead of the evaluator's. Both are fixed with gates, and your exact-conditioning proposal, tested as a standalone computation, removes the horizon residue completely. Details below, then my view of the theory proposal, which you had not seen when you wrote the reaction, then the questions.

## 1. Mean-freezing at hard edges: wrong, and fixed

Your example holds: $\xi = \varepsilon(Z^2 - \tfrac12)$, $S = \mathbf 1_{\xi > 0}$, width $\sqrt2\,\varepsilon$, mean $\varepsilon/2$, exact value $\operatorname{erfc}(1/2) = 0.4795001$ for every $\varepsilon$. What the compiler actually did was fold the count to a number at trace time (a zero gradient meant "constant" there, before any width was computed) and return the step at the centre, $0$. So there were two freeze rules and I had corrected the later one.

The fix has three parts. The tracer folds a count to a number only when both its gradient and its Hessian vanish. A count that is mostly curvature keeps its axis, and a hard picture on it is integrated as the coverage of the quadratic form, in the way you describe: rotate to the Hessian's eigenvectors, condition on one coordinate, and in the other the count is a quadratic whose sublevel sets are intervals, so $F(c) = P(\xi \le c)$ is a sum of normal distribution differences; the conditioning integral is cut where roots appear or vanish (the discriminant's zeros, explicit) and where the inert-direction indicator jumps; the picture is sampled over the count's excursion with its jumps and kinks located by bisection, and $\mathbb E\,p(\xi) = \int p\,dF$ is taken segment by segment, the square-root singularities of $F$ at the count's extremes by adaptive Simpson. Edge axes take this path whenever curvature dominates their width; periodic axes when their pictures jump within the excursion. A smooth picture of a curved count stays spectral, where it is exact, and you are right that a stationary quadratic phase suppresses harmonics as $k^{-1/2}$ rather than not at all; I had written that carelessly, and the practical point stands: for a hard picture the periodised series converges as $K^{-1/2}$ and the coverage integral is the right tool.

Gates: your example at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}, 0.1, 1$ with a step, a sign and a relu, within $2\times10^{-9}$ (step) and $7\times10^{-8}\varepsilon$ (relu, against $\mathbb E(Z^2-\tfrac12)^+ = 0.6791413$); and rotated rank-two counts (a saddle, a bowl and a ridge with a rate, both curvatures and a cross term) with a step and a relu against a two-dimensional midpoint reference converged to $10^{-8}$, within $2\times10^{-5}$ for the steps (the reference's own accuracy on a discontinuity) and $2\times10^{-6}$ for the relus. Two bugs were found by those gates and would not have been found by your one-dimensional example: a located jump, treated as a segment of length $10^{-16}$, gave a chord of slope $10^{16}$ and a catastrophic cancellation, so $P(\xi > 0.2)$ came out $-0.38$; and the jump detector compared each step with the median step, which is zero when most of the excursion is flat, so every sloped segment was a jump and a relu's kink was never located. The ridge relu was $5\times10^{-5}$ off until the detector compared each step with its neighbours. I mention them because they are the kind of thing your test protocol (adversarial cases for boundaries and stationary phases) is designed to catch, and it did.

One approximation remains and is stated in the code and the notes: the coverage replaces the hard factor by its expectation, so a term's other factors are treated as independent of the edge's region. That is exact for your example and for any single-factor term, and it is the same independence the freeze had assumed; the correlated case needs the Gaussian integral of a phase over an interval, which is the complex error function along the conditioned direction, and it is not built. Davies's algorithm I read: for our two-dimensional counts the conditional integral is simpler and exact, and the chi-square inversion becomes the tool when counts have more inputs (time, more than two pixel coordinates).

## 2. The multiplier-weighted remainder: retracted

Your example settles it. $S = \cos(4Z + \tfrac12 Z^2)$, $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$: the affine prediction is $e^{-8} = 3.35\times10^{-4}$, the exact quadratic expectation is $\mathrm{Re}[(1-i)^{-1/2}e^{-8/(1-i)}] = -0.01376$ (closed form and a fine quadrature agree to eight digits), and the proposed weighted bound is $1.7\times10^{-4}$. The oscillatory factor cannot be averaged separately from the position-dependent perturbation, and I now see the mechanism: the quadratic phase detunes the Gaussian's suppression, so the multiplier is not a bound on anything once curvature is present. I had actually computed this effect the same morning for the harmonic caps and failed to apply it to the remainder. The compiler is unaffected, because it integrates the quadratic phase exactly; the theorem's hypothesis stays summable coefficients; and there is no remainder bound of that form for hard profiles. The honest replacement is the one you propose from the observation measure: for a hard profile the error is the mass of the pushed-forward pixel that the phase model displaces across a jump, $\sum_j |\Delta_j|\,P(\text{the model's error at the jump exceeds the distance to it})$. That is what the coverage integral computes when the model is quadratic, and it is what exact conditioning drives to zero when the geometry allows. I will state it as the target of a bound, not as a bound.

## 3. Exact vertical conditioning: it removes the horizon residue

I ran your proposal as a standalone computation on the circles shader before touching the compiler. At fixed $Y$ the perspective is affine in $X$, so the inside-set of the discs is a union of intervals in $X$ and the inner integral is a sum of normal distribution differences; the outer integral over $Y$ is a fine midpoint rule, because the integrand oscillates at the $t$-count's rate, about a hundred cycles across the pixel at $y = 5$. Against a two-dimensional midpoint reference converged to $10^{-7}$:

| pixel | compiler minus reference | conditioned minus reference |
|---|---|---|
| $(240,5)$ | $-1.61\times10^{-3}$ | $6\times10^{-8}$ |
| $(300,12)$ | $-2.7\times10^{-4}$ | $2\times10^{-7}$ |
| $(240,20)$ | $-9.9\times10^{-5}$ | $10^{-9}$ |
| $(30,5)$ | $-3\times10^{-8}$ | $3\times10^{-8}$ |

So the whole of the horizon residue is the perspective's Taylor remainder, as the earlier sigma-scaling suggested and the mixture experiment could not show, and the remedy is exact where the second-order model was not. Your guiding sentence, look for an exactly eliminable coordinate before raising the order, is the right one, and there is a sharper form of it for this geometry: in coordinates $(X, v)$ with $v = 1/d$ the state map is bilinear, so second-order jets in $(X, v)$ are exact for the geometry and every recipe's phase is linear in $v$; only the pixel's measure is non-Gaussian in $v$, with an explicit density. The compiler change is therefore to trace in $(X, v)$, keep the closed form in $X$, and integrate over $v$ against that density, one Fourier integral per recipe (one FFT per $k_s$ serves every $k_t$). Near $d = 0$ I will do what the benchmark's reference does, evaluate the formula on both sides of the horizon, and say so; a renderer would clip, and the two conventions differ only within a few $\sigma$ of the horizon row.

The cubic reduction is correct and useful, and I take the point that no Airy implementation is needed. Its one complication is the one you name: the null direction of the cubic tensor depends on the recipe when the counts do not share their non-affine direction. For the perspective they do, and exact conditioning supersedes the cubic model there; the reduction is the fallback for counts that are themselves cubic in the surface coordinates.

## 4. Control variates with joint covariance

Agreed, and this is the part of the reaction that changes the plan most. The window covariance of two recipes is $m_{k-\ell} - m_k\bar m_\ell$ up to the phase, the same integral that gives their means, and it is the diffusion calculus's product rule in the semigroup language. I had written only the diagonal of it. Your cancelling pair, $\cos\omega z - \cos(\omega+\varepsilon)z$ with $\sigma\omega \gg 1$ and $\sigma\varepsilon \ll 1$, shows why the diagonal is not enough: the two fast waves have small joint variance and subtracting one of them leaves a residual of variance one half. So the selection is $\beta = G^\dagger b$ over the chosen span, with $G$ from the phase structure and $b$ the covariance with the shader model, and the untraced part of the shader enters only through a pilot sample. The operational rule you give is the one I will follow: subtract at the sample points exactly the finite expansion whose mean is used, so that the estimator is unbiased for that $q$ by construction, and approximation quality decides efficiency and nothing else. For coefficient jets the covariance needs the oscillatory Gaussian moments to degree four, which are further derivatives of the same quadratic integral; I have $I_0, I_1, I_2$ and will add $I_3, I_4$. The cost model with setup, per-sample and per-control costs is what the comparison will optimise, and the three references (control variate polynomial chaos, Leluc, Portier and Segers on control selection, Owen's chapter) are the right prior art; the specific contribution to test is compiler-extracted joint oscillatory controls, as you say.

## 5. On the theory proposal

You had not seen my view of the first note when you wrote the reaction, so briefly.

The distribution-of-states formulation is the compiler's architecture stated without the Gaussian approximation, and "choose the representation according to what survives observation" is the best one-line description of it I have. The heat semigroup on the torus is the multiplier theorem relabelled, exactly and usefully: it connects the work to Gaussian scale-space, and its product rule gives the variance of a pixel's samples from the same operator that gives the mean, which is the matrix $G$ of Section 4. The caution is that the semigroup exists only while the Jacobian is constant across the reach; with curvature, blurring twice on the torus is not blurring once at the larger scale, and the object that evolves autonomously with scale is the Gaussian in input space. So the elegant structure lives where the theory was already easy, and the hard rows are where it stops, as you say at the end of the note.

Averaging out the fast coordinate first is the conditional-expectation limit that your own point 3 criticised; the exact version is the multiplier, which discards no coordinate. What the coordinate change buys is real but different: enumerating recipes and building closure transforms in the Lagrange–Gauss reduced basis makes the retained ellipse an axis-aligned box, replacing the compiler's range-growth heuristics with something exact and shrinking the two-axis transforms from $K \times K$ to $K_{\text{slow}} \times K_{\text{fast}}$.

The composition operator is the most valuable part. I checked what fails today on fire with bumps: the compiler declines the shift and stops with "residual of four axes not supported". The four are the two bump counts, the pattern count carrying the parallax field, and the palette count; but the pattern count's base is the bump's own count with a field on it, which in your view is a closure over the two bump axes and not a fourth axis. The residual set drops to three, which the generic path supports, and the shift table reappears as the family $f(u + \theta H(u))$ tabulated in $\theta$ on a shared axis. That is the first test you proposed and it is the next thing I build after the exact conditioning.

## 6. What is accepted without comment

"Exact" reserved for identities and specified models, with proven bounds, error estimates and empirical discrepancies kept apart, and the transform and interpolation errors and the aggregate of omitted terms in the budget. Parameter sweeps plus a small frozen random grammar with every case and exclusion rule published, and adversarial tests for cancellation, boundary proximity and stationary phase. Candidate generation as the priced merit's role, the record property explicitly empirical, and your added point that even exact $1/n$ envelopes do not make least merit equal to greatest filtered contribution because the scale enters the multiplier and not the merit. The duty null and a compact cancellation example in the main paper, the rest of the moiré theory separate.
