\section{The instrument}
\label{sec:instrument}

Every result in this paper was found in a drawing tool before it was
proved, and measured in the tool after. \Moire\ is a web application that
draws superpositions of families the way this paper describes them. Each
layer is a count $\cnt_i$ and a stroke profile; at every pixel the count is
evaluated --- by a formula for the exact and winding rungs, by a certified
search for the fold rung --- and the picture on the torus is looked up
there and then. No layer is ever drawn into an intermediate image and
resampled, so a superposition has no resolution of its own: zooming asks
the same counts at new points, and cannot invent a beat that the geometry
does not have or destroy one it does. Fourteen families ship, sorted by
rung: on the exact rung, parallel lines, concentric circles, squares,
triangles and hexagons, the Archimedean and the logarithmic spiral, the
wave, the parabola and the hyperbola, and three lattices (square,
triangular, hexagonal) with their tilings; on the winding rung, the rays;
and on the fold rung, the walking versions of the concentric shapes. A
field language adds any function of position to a count.

Three of its views are this paper's theorems running
(Figure~\ref{fig:instrument}). The \emph{pooled} view is
Theorem~\ref{thm:sweep}: the slide average, integrated in closed form.
Along a slide every layer's coverage is its stroke profile, a window with
soft edges, evaluated at a distance that changes linearly with the slide,
so the coverage is a cubic polynomial on each stretch between corners and
the corners are known from the count; the average is therefore a sum of
integrals of polynomials with known endpoints, exact at every zoom, with no
sampling and no noise, and it runs at the frame rate (certified against a
$65{,}536$-point brute-force integral by \texttt{exactsweep.mjs}). The
\emph{ratio} view is Definition~\ref{def:merit}: the priced merit of the
winning recipe at every pixel, found by the lattice reduction of
Proposition~\ref{prop:selection}, drawn as a map that is dark where a beat
must form; for a first-order recipe it is the $\het$ of
\S\ref{sec:slow}. A \emph{square-law} toggle is
Theorem~\ref{thm:universality}: the drawing squared before it is averaged,
so that Corollary~\ref{cor:soft} can be watched. The strokes' soft edges
are a fixed width in pixels, so zooming out makes them a larger fraction
of the pitch, and the null of a $2{:}1$ pair at coarse duty one half, flat
under the linear pooled view at every zoom, reopens under the square-law
toggle as the zoom widens the edges.

The field language also runs the theory backwards. A picture with
brightness $g$ is a field: give a copy of a layer the index shift
$D=\tfrac12(1-g)$, read from the picture in the copy's own coordinates,
and the tent of Corollary~\ref{cor:cases} says what any pooling observer
sees of the pair. The strokes align where the picture is white and
interleave where it is black, so in register the overlay is the picture,
at half contrast, and nothing else (Figure~\ref{fig:inverse}), grey
levels included, since the tent is linear in the offset up to one half.
The amount is a dial: a whole member of shift at black is invisible inside
a dark region, so only its edges, where the shift passes through one half,
stay dark, and a silhouette is drawn as an outline. Out of register the
same two layers are the classical band moir\'e with the picture riding on
it. Moved a quarter of a pitch, the picture rides on the bands two ring
families make; turned or moved further, the bands narrow and the picture
dissolves into them, and as the motion closes the bands widen until one of
them is the whole frame and the picture is back. The construction is one
line because the count is the object: the picture is a slow combination
chosen by hand, and the base family is free --- concentric circles in the
figure, which no straight-grating method reaches.

The picture can also be shared between the two layers, and the theory
says exactly how well. A crisp picture needs the difference of the two
shifts to jump by one half at every edge, so at least one layer jumps
there, and the least either can jump is a quarter, in opposite directions:
half the shift on each layer. Alone, each layer then changes only where
the picture changes, by at most a quarter of a pitch, and the tool bends
that quarter over half a pitch rather than kinking it, so the members make
a gentle S and the overlay's edges soften by the same half pitch, a hair at
the scale of a picture (Figure~\ref{fig:inverse}, bottom row). Hiding the
picture from a single layer altogether is possible --- it is visual
cryptography~\cite{NaorShamir1994Visual} in the count's currency, and
Desmedt and Van Le~\cite{DesmedtVanLe2000Moire} did it with two random
gratings --- but only by jittering every cell of both layers by a quarter
pitch, since a jump that is nowhere else is always visible where it is;
the tool can draw that weave, and does not offer it, because it costs the
layers their look. In \Moire\ the picture is the Picture button of the
field editor, and its Share row puts it on one layer or on two, the second
being the picture's unmodulated twin.

The theory also runs against the tool's own pixels. A pixel is a comb
family, and a family drawn at two pixels a period beats with it: zooming
out on concentric circles once drew the star of hyperbolae of
Figure~\ref{fig:alias}, and one pixel a period drew black, because a
hairline rule that widens thin strokes so they survive the screen had
widened them past the whole pitch. Both are the strobe of
\S\ref{sec:stations} happening to the instrument. The cure is the window
multiplier theorem taken literally: the pixel is a window, and it shows
the composite of the two-valued drawing averaged over that window. For
one or two families the tool now computes that average exactly, at every
zoom, in place of the point-sampled drawing. The window is an isotropic
Gaussian; along each family's normal it is a Gaussian of the same width,
and along two normals the pair is bivariate normal with correlation the
cosine of the angle between them. A family inks where the displacement
along its normal falls within a half-stroke of a member, so each ink
pattern's probability is a sum of error functions: exact in one dimension
where the normals are within twenty-five degrees of parallel, which is
the moir\'e case, and a short Gauss--Legendre integral of the conditional
where they cross. The window is as narrow as the drawn edge at seven
pixels a period and coarser, half a pixel by four, and 0.9 of a pixel by
two, where a stripe sits at the buffer's Nyquist frequency and a narrower
window would pass a quarter of it to beat with the pixel grid. Nothing
wider: a stripe the screen can show keeps its contrast, only what would
alias goes, two strokes in register are the light of one, and the beat
emerges by eye, as it does on paper. A first cut had integrated a box
along one direction with every family in lockstep --- the slide average
of Theorem~\ref{thm:sweep}, exact only where the families run parallel
--- and drew rosettes where two ring families cross; a second erased every
carrier under a window half a pitch wide, which is the envelope's
observer, not the pixel's, and blurred what the screen could still show.
Three and four families use the same theorem as a Fourier series, each
term under the window at its own two-dimensional frequency, handed over
from the drawing where the harmonics reach the comb, and their envelopes,
while a hand is on the view, are drawn by that series with the slide's
sinc on each term. Every width that sets a mean is measured in the pixels
of the screen at rest; a coarse interaction buffer sees the same drawing
through a wider window, with the same light.

The envelope of a pair is a case of the same rule, and the cheapest one.
Its slide average depends on the two counts only through the character
the schedule holds fixed, so it is a periodic function of one number, the
tent of \S\ref{sec:stations}, composed with that character's count map.
The tool now tabulates the tent once a frame by the certified integrator,
512 samples a cycle and one row for each character the scan can pick,
and a pixel reads the tent at its own count through its window, a
Gaussian along the count of the pixel's width times the character's
gradient. Where the integrator ran once per pixel it runs once per texel;
the fast beats at a ring pair's foci, which a reduced buffer had
point-sampled into rosettes, are averaged to the tent's mean where they
pass the pixel's resolving power; and the frame drawn under a moving hand
is the frame drawn after it lifts.

Every view is a measurement with a gate, in two different senses that
should not be confused. The count solver for the fold rung is checked
against a brute-force twin that tries every member, so its answers are
checked against truth. The renders are checked against golden images
through the real graphics device on every change, which catches any change
in what the tool draws but would preserve a wrong picture faithfully; the
goldens are trusted because the scripts of Table~\ref{tab:predictions}
check what they show against the theorems. And every number in this paper
is written into the text by the program that fails if an experiment does.
The tool, its source, and every script named in this paper are public at
\todo{URL, to be supplied by the author}; the systems paper describes the
engineering, and is where the proofs of \S\ref{sec:counting}'s closed
forms live.

\begin{tryit}{the three views}
In \Moire, make two layers of parallel lines at pitches $15$ and $5$ and
turn one by two degrees. The drawn view shows a fine texture with broad
bands in it; the pooled view keeps the bands and drops the texture; the
ratio view is dark exactly where the pooled view has bands. Now change the
coarse pitch to $16$: the bands change shape, the dark region moves before
they do, and the station table of \S\ref{sec:selection} says which recipe
you are looking at.
\end{tryit}
